Using a Medical Marijuana Card Outside Your Home State

A Michigan medical card doesn't travel the way a driver's license does. Here's how other states handle visiting patients, where the neighbors stand and how to travel smart.

By the High Society teamPublished October 24, 20267 min read

07min readCannabis 101
In this article

  2. Why states differ
  3. Four kinds of reciprocity, with examples
  4. Neighboring states Michigan patients visit most
  5. Michigan's own rules for visiting patients
  6. Travel tips for Michigan patients
  7. When your card isn't honored: the adult-use route
  8. Frequently asked questions

Key takeaways

  • Reciprocity has two parts: whether a state protects your possession and whether its dispensaries will sell to you.
  • States fall into four rough groups: card accepted at the counter, visitor registration, limited recognition and no recognition.
  • As of October 2026, Indiana and Wisconsin have no medical program, Illinois issues cards to its own residents, and Ohio allows only cannabis from its own licensed dispensaries or legal home grows.
  • Never carry cannabis across a state line, even between two states with medical programs.
  • Adults 21 and older can often take the adult-use route instead, within that state's visitor limits.

Medical marijuana reciprocity means another state honors your medical card the way your home state does. In practice, it varies widely. Some states let visiting patients buy at their dispensaries with a valid card and photo ID, some make you register first, and many don't recognize out-of-state cards at all. Here's how it works, where Michigan's neighbors stand and how to travel without legal trouble.

What medical marijuana reciprocity means: possession vs. purchase

Reciprocity covers two separate questions, and it helps to keep them apart:

  • Possession. Will the state protect you for having medical cannabis while you're there?
  • Purchase. Will its dispensaries sell to you as a patient?

A state can say yes to one and no to the other. Michigan says yes to both. Its medical law gives a valid card from another state the same force as a Michigan card. Licensed medical provisioning centers may also sell to visiting patients who show an unexpired card and photo ID. Our guide to Michigan medical marijuana reciprocity covers the details for visitors.

Neither question covers the trip itself. Reciprocity is about what happens inside a state's borders, not about carrying cannabis across them.

Why states differ

Every medical program is written by its own state, so the details rarely line up:

  • Conditions. Each state keeps its own list of qualifying conditions, and some honor visitors only if their condition also qualifies there.
  • Residency. Many programs issue cards only to their own residents.
  • Paperwork. Some states accept your card at the counter, while others want an application and a fee first.
  • Federal backdrop. As of October 2026, federal law places only marijuana drugs approved by the FDA and marijuana covered by a state medical license in Schedule III. All other marijuana remains in Schedule I. There's no national medical card, and the 2026 federal change doesn't say patients may carry cannabis between states.

Programs change often, so use any list as a starting point and check the destination state's official site before you go.

Four kinds of reciprocity, with examples

Here's a simple way to sort states, with examples as of October 2026:

TypeWhat it means for a visiting patientExample
Card accepted at the counterShow a valid home-state card and photo ID, then buyMichigan, at licensed medical provisioning centers
Visitor registrationApply and pay before you can buyOklahoma: a 30-day out-of-state license for $100 plus a credit card processing fee
Limited recognitionExtra conditions on who qualifiesArkansas: 90-day visiting approval for $50, and your condition must also qualify in Arkansas
No recognitionNo path for out-of-state patients to buyIllinois issues medical cards to Illinois residents; Indiana and Wisconsin have no medical program

A few notes on those examples:

  • Oklahoma's out-of-state license requires a medical card issued by your state government, plus proof of identity and a photo. It lets you buy and use medical cannabis in Oklahoma for 30 days.
  • Arkansas requires a card or its equivalent from another state, takes up to 14 days to process, and has approved visitors print their own card.
  • Illinois lets visitors 21 and older possess adult-use cannabis within its non-resident limits, even though its medical program is for residents.

Neighboring states Michigan patients visit most

Michigan borders Indiana, Ohio and Wisconsin, and Illinois is a short drive away on I-94. None of them is a place to bring Michigan cannabis. Here's where each stands as of October 2026:

  • Indiana. There's no adult-use or medical marijuana program, and possessing any amount is a crime, starting as a misdemeanor punishable by up to 180 days in jail. Our Indiana law guide has more.
  • Ohio. Ohio has a medical program and has sold adult-use cannabis to adults 21 and older since August 2024. Since its Senate Bill 56 took effect on March 20, 2026, Ohio allows possession only of cannabis bought from an Ohio-licensed dispensary or legally grown at home. Michigan purchases can't legally come along. Check with Ohio's Division of Cannabis Control before counting on a Michigan card there.
  • Illinois. Adults 21 and older can buy, and non-residents may possess up to 30 grams of flower, 500 mg of THC in infused products and 5 grams of concentrate. The state's medical cards go to Illinois residents. See our Illinois law guide.
  • Wisconsin. Neither adult-use nor medical marijuana is legal, and a first possession conviction can bring up to six months in jail and a fine of up to $1,000.

Michigan's own rules for visiting patients

If you're visiting Michigan with a card from home, the basics are simple:

  • Bring your valid, unexpired card and a photo ID. Stores check ID before every sale.
  • Buy at a licensed medical provisioning center, where Michigan's medical limits apply: 2.5 ounces a day and 10 ounces a month.
  • Visiting-patient purchases count as medical sales, so they skip the 10% adult-use excise tax. The 6% sales tax still applies.
  • Michigan medical cards themselves go only to Michigan residents.

High Society welcomes medical patients 18 and older with a valid card. If yours comes from another state, call your store before you visit. Michigan residents can learn how to apply or renew in our medical card guide.

Travel tips for Michigan patients

  • Leave cannabis behind at state lines. Michigan's protections stop at the state line, and no federal law allows carrying cannabis across it, even between two states with medical programs. As our stores put it: enjoy responsibly in Michigan, and don't travel across state lines with cannabis.
  • Keep your card and ID with you. In Michigan, a patient is protected from arrest only by showing a valid registry card along with photo ID. An application or physician form doesn't count.
  • Check local limits and consumption laws. Possession limits, product caps and the places you can use cannabis all change at the border. Illinois, for example, requires cannabis in a moving car to stay sealed in child-resistant, odor-proof original packaging.
  • Think twice about flying. TSA says its officers focus on security, but they refer anything illegal they find to law enforcement, and the final call rests with the officer. Federal law still prohibits adult-use marijuana, and your destination's laws apply when you land. Our guide to flying with cannabis covers more.
  • Never drive impaired. Michigan bans using cannabis while driving, and impaired driving is illegal in every state.

When your card isn't honored: the adult-use route

If you're 21 or older, the adult-use route is often simpler. Michigan and Illinois both sell adult-use cannabis to adults 21 and older, visitors included, with no medical card needed. In Michigan, that means showing government-issued photo ID at every sale. Expect adult-use taxes and visitor limits: in Illinois, non-residents may possess half of what residents can.

If you're 18 to 20, adult-use shopping isn't an option, so you'll need a state that honors your card or registers visiting patients. Wherever you buy, what you buy stays in that state.

Planning a Michigan trip? Our Michigan cannabis laws guide covers limits and the places you can consume.

Frequently asked questions

Does Indiana accept Michigan medical cards?

No. Indiana has no medical or adult-use marijuana program, and possessing any amount is a crime there, as of October 2026. A Michigan card offers no protection in Indiana, so never carry Michigan cannabis across the state line. Hoosiers 21 and older can shop adult-use in Michigan, but what they buy has to stay in Michigan.

Can I fly with medical marijuana?

It's risky, and a medical card doesn't make it legal wherever you land. TSA says screening focuses on security, but officers refer anything illegal they find to law enforcement, and the final decision rests with the officer. Federal law still bans adult-use marijuana, and the laws where you land apply too. The safest plan is to leave it at home.

Do adult-use states honor medical cards?

Not automatically. Adult-use sales and medical reciprocity are separate questions. Illinois, for example, sells to visitors 21 and older but issues medical cards to its own residents. If you're 21 or older, you can usually shop adult-use within visitor limits. If you're 18 to 20, look for a state that honors your card or registers visiting patients.

Can I drive across state lines with medical cannabis?

No. Michigan's protections stop at the state line, and no federal law permits carrying cannabis across one, even between two medical states. Neighbors add their own rules: Indiana and Wisconsin make possession a crime, and Ohio allows only cannabis bought from its own licensed dispensaries or grown legally at home. Leave it at home before you travel.
