How Patients Use Each Cannabis Product: RSO, Vape Carts, Capsules and More

A plain-language guide for Michigan patients and new shoppers: what THC, CBD and terpenes are, and how capsules, edibles, flower, vapes, RSO, tinctures and topicals work.

Key takeaways

  • This guide is education, not medical advice: decide with your certifying physician whether cannabis fits your care.
  • THC is intoxicating and CBD is not; terpenes shape aroma and flavor, and research on their effects is early.
  • Capsules, edibles and RSO are slow: you may not feel them for up to 2 hours, and they can keep working for many hours, so start low and wait.
  • Inhaled products act within minutes and are easier to pace, while concentrates are far stronger than flower.
  • Michigan patients 18 and older can shop at High Society with a valid registry card and a photo ID.

This guide explains how to use medical cannabis products, one format at a time. It starts with the compounds that matter most, THC, CBD and terpenes, then walks through capsules and edibles, flower, vape carts, concentrates, RSO, tinctures and topicals. It's written for Michigan medical patients and for anyone new to shopping at a dispensary.

How to use medical cannabis products: read this first

This is general education, not medical advice. Whether cannabis belongs in your care, which form suits you and how much to take are decisions for your certifying physician or another clinician who knows your history.

Talk with a clinician before you start if you're pregnant or breastfeeding, take other medications, or have a heart or mental health condition. Using cannabis during pregnancy or while breastfeeding may harm the baby, and cannabis can interact with other drugs.

Our budtenders can explain how products work, read labels with you and compare formats. They aren't medical professionals, though, and they can't advise you about a health condition. If you still need a card, start with our Michigan medical card guide. For a deeper reference on cannabinoids and product types, see the High Society patient guide.

THC, CBD and terpenes in plain words

THC

THC is the cannabinoid behind the intoxicating side of cannabis. It can temporarily alter your mood, thoughts and perceptions, which is the high people describe. In raw flower, most of it exists as THCA, an acid form that turns into THC when it's heated by smoking, vaping or baking.

How THC feels depends on the amount, the product and the person. Some people feel relaxed or upbeat. Others feel anxious or uneasy, which is more likely after a large amount or a strong product. That's why starting low matters: Canadian public health guidance suggests a first edible hold no more than 2.5 mg of THC. Our THC dosage guide explains how to find your amount.

CBD

CBD is not intoxicating like THC. People use it on its own or in products that pair it with THC. It can cause side effects of its own, including drowsiness, decreased appetite, vomiting and diarrhea.

Where you buy CBD matters. Testing has found CBD products with different amounts than their labels show, and some sold as CBD have contained THC. CBD products on a licensed Michigan dispensary's shelf have passed state-required testing, and the label lists the THC and CBD content. Our guide to cannabinoids compares CBD with THC and the minor compounds.

Terpenes

Terpenes are the aroma compounds behind a strain's scent, and many other plants make them too. Myrcene has an earthy note also found in hops, limonene smells of citrus peel, beta-caryophyllene is peppery, linalool is floral like lavender, and pinene smells like a pine forest.

You'll hear claims that certain terpenes relax or energize you. Research on that is still early, so think of a terpene profile as a guide to aroma and flavor first. Our terpene guide goes deeper.

Capsules and edibles

Swallowed cannabis takes the long way through your body. Your gut absorbs it, and your liver turns some of the THC into a stronger form, 11-hydroxy-THC, before it reaches your brain, so the effects arrive slowly and stay a long time. Canadian public health guidance puts the wait at up to 2 hours before you feel an edible and up to 4 hours before it reaches full effect. The effects can last 4 to 12 hours, and some can linger for up to 24.

Capsules hold a measured amount of cannabis oil in a pill. They're tasteless and consistent, which makes them easy to track. Michigan caps adult-use capsules at 10 mg of THC per dose, while products made for registered patients can contain up to 100 mg per dose.

Edibles include gummies, chocolates, baked goods and drinks. Adult-use edibles in Michigan can contain no more than 10 mg of THC per serving and 200 mg per package. Edibles made for registered patients can hold up to 50 mg per serving, so read the serving size before you eat.

A few habits help with both:

  • If you're new, start at 2.5 mg of THC or below.
  • Wait at least 2 hours before deciding whether to take more, and remember full effects can take up to 4.
  • Avoid mixing cannabis with alcohol or other substances.
  • Keep products in their packaging and stored where children and pets can't reach them. Edibles can look like ordinary food.

Flower and other ways to inhale

When you inhale cannabis, THC passes from your lungs into your blood, and you'll usually feel it within minutes. That makes it easier to pace than an edible: you can stop after a puff or two and see how you feel.

  • Pre-rolls are ready-made joints. Some are infused with concentrate or rolled in kief, which makes them much stronger, so check the label.
  • Pipes are small, simple and easy to clean. You pack a pinch of ground flower into the bowl and light it.
  • Water pipes, often called bongs, pass smoke through water to cool it. Hits can be large, so go slowly.
  • Rolling papers let you roll a joint at whatever size suits you.
  • Dry-herb vaporizers heat flower without burning it, so you inhale vapor instead of smoke.

Smoking any product, including cannabis, can damage your lungs and heart, according to the CDC. If your lungs are sensitive, ask your clinician about non-inhaled options. And never drive after using cannabis: driving under the influence is against the law in Michigan.

Vape cartridges: 510 threads, fill levels and how long a cart lasts

A vape cartridge, or cart, is a small tank of cannabis oil with a mouthpiece on one end and a heating coil inside. You attach it to a battery and draw.

What is a 510 cartridge? The number names the most common screw thread for carts and batteries. If both pieces are 510-thread, they usually fit together across brands. Many batteries let you pick a lower voltage, and lab tests have found fewer harmful byproducts at lower settings.

Fill levels. Carts are sold by weight, often half a gram or a full gram, and the label shows the net weight. Go by that number rather than by how full the tank looks.

How long a cart lasts. It depends on the cart's size and on how often and how long you draw. A half-gram cart holds half as much oil as a full gram, and short draws of two or three seconds go further than long pulls. Store carts upright and away from heat.

Buy carts only from a licensed retailer, where every product's label names the lab that tested it.

Concentrates and the devices that use them

Concentrates gather the plant's cannabinoids and terpenes into a small, potent form, from glassy shatter to whipped badder and pressed rosin. People use them in a dab rig or electronic rig, in a dab pen, or sprinkled on flower in a bowl.

Because they're so strong, concentrates are rarely a starting point for new patients. Dabbing can deliver a large amount of THC very quickly, which raises the risk of unwanted effects. If you've built some tolerance and want to try them, start with the smallest portion you can.

RSO: swallowed, under the tongue and on the skin

RSO, short for Rick Simpson Oil, is a thick, dark, whole-plant cannabis oil named for the medical cannabis activist who created it. It's usually high in THC and often comes in a small oral syringe. Our RSO guide covers its history and how to read its label.

  • Swallowed. RSO is often swallowed, on its own or mixed into food, and it acts like an edible: slow to start and long-lasting. It's so concentrated that even a tiny drop can hold far more than the 2.5 mg of THC suggested for a first edible, so check the label's THC content and ask your clinician how to measure a dose.
  • Under the tongue. Some people hold a small amount under the tongue for a minute before swallowing. Timing varies, because some of the oil is still swallowed.
  • On the skin. Some people apply RSO to the skin. Research on that use is limited, and RSO isn't the same as a topical made for skin.

You'll find big claims about RSO online. Strong human evidence for them is lacking, so talk with your clinician before relying on it for anything.

Tinctures and topicals

Tinctures are cannabis extracts in an alcohol or oil base, sold in dropper bottles. The label lists the THC and CBD content, and the dropper's markings help you measure small amounts. Many people hold a dose under the tongue briefly, then swallow. Whatever is swallowed behaves like an edible, so wait before taking more.

Topicals are lotions, balms and salves you rub into the skin. They're meant to act where they're applied rather than throughout the body. Transdermal patches are different: they're designed to carry cannabinoids through the skin into the bloodstream, so read their labels as you would an edible's.

Tinctures and topicals appear on some High Society store menus. Selection varies, so check your store before you visit.

Shopping as a patient at High Society

Medical patients 18 and older with a valid medical marijuana card are welcome at High Society. Bring your registry card and a valid photo ID, as Michigan requires at the time of purchase. Without a card, shopping is for adults 21 and older.

Order ahead online for in-store or curbside pickup, or browse in person with a budtender who can explain formats, labels and serving sizes. Members of The Society, our free loyalty program, earn points on qualifying purchases. Please consume responsibly, and store every product where children and pets can't get to it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way for a new patient to start with cannabis?

Start with a conversation with your certifying physician. Then pick one format with predictable dosing, such as a low-dose edible, capsule or tincture, or a single puff of flower. For anything you swallow, begin at no more than 2.5 mg of THC, and wait at least 2 hours before taking more. Jot down each product, the amount and the way it felt.

Can I take THC without feeling high?

Not reliably. THC is the cannabinoid that causes the high, so any active amount can produce some effect, though very small amounts are felt less. Some people look at CBD-dominant products instead, since CBD is not intoxicating like THC. Talk with your clinician about which balance suits you, and start with the smallest amount.

What is RSO and how is it used?

RSO, or Rick Simpson Oil, is a thick, whole-plant cannabis oil, usually high in THC and often sold in a small syringe. It's usually swallowed, alone or in food, so it acts like an edible and may not be felt for up to 2 hours. Some people hold it under the tongue or apply it to the skin. It's so concentrated that even a tiny drop can hold more than a beginner's 2.5 mg, so ask your clinician how to measure a dose.

What is a 510 cartridge?

It's a vape cart with the most common screw thread, called 510, which fits most vape batteries across brands. The cart holds cannabis oil and a heating coil, and the battery powers it. Carts are often sold in half-gram or full-gram sizes, and the label shows the net weight.

Can medical patients under 21 shop at High Society?

Yes, with a card. High Society welcomes medical patients 18 and older who have a valid medical marijuana card. Bring the card and a valid photo ID when you shop. Without a valid card, shopping is for adults 21 and older, so renew your card before it expires.
