Disposable Cannabis Vapes Explained: Hardware, Extracts and Choosing One

What's inside an all-in-one cannabis vape, how disposables stack up against cartridges, which label details matter and how to get rid of an empty one safely.

By the High Society teamPublished October 24, 20267 min read

07min readCannabis 101
In this article

  2. Why people choose disposables
  3. Hardware features worth knowing
  4. Distillate, live resin and live rosin disposables
  5. Disposable vs. cartridge: which suits you?

  7. Troubleshooting: clogs, charging and the last few puffs
  8. Disposal: batteries and e-waste
  9. Frequently asked questions

Key takeaways

  • Disposables put a battery, a heating element and cannabis oil in one sealed unit that you use until it's empty.
  • Disposables trade convenience and variety for a higher cost per gram and more waste than a cartridge and battery.
  • Oil type matters as much as potency: distillate is refined oil, while live resin and live rosin start from fresh-frozen plants.
  • Buy only from licensed stores: during the lung-injury outbreak tied to vaping in 2019 and early 2020, most cases were linked to THC vapes, especially ones from informal sources.
  • Empty vapes belong at household hazardous waste or battery collection, not in the trash or the recycling bin.

Disposable vape pens are all-in-one cannabis vapes: a battery, a heating element and a reservoir of cannabis oil sealed into a single device. There's nothing to fill, match or set up. When the oil is gone, the whole device is done, and it needs proper disposal. Here's how disposables work, how they compare with cartridges and what to check before you buy one.

What disposable vape pens are

A disposable has the same basic parts as any oil vape pen, built into one body:

  • Battery. Powers the device. Many recharge through a USB port.
  • Heating element. Often ceramic, it warms the oil until it turns to vapor, without burning it.
  • Oil reservoir. Comes prefilled with a set amount of cannabis extract.
  • Mouthpiece and airflow. Most are draw-activated, so you simply inhale.

Unlike a cartridge setup, you can't swap in new oil or move the battery to another tank. Our guide to vape pens breaks down each part in more detail.

Why people choose disposables

  • Convenience. There's no cartridge to screw on and no battery to match.
  • Discretion. They're small, and they make vapor instead of smoke.
  • Variety. A disposable lets you try a new strain or extract type without buying a battery.
  • Consistency. Each device is built around its own oil and heating element, which takes the guesswork out of pairing parts.

The trade-offs are cost per gram and waste: when the oil is gone, the battery goes with it.

Hardware features worth knowing

Hardware varies from device to device. Features to look for include:

  • Ceramic heating elements. Many disposables use ceramic cores, which makers say heat thick oil more evenly than older wick designs.
  • Preheat. On devices that have it, a short warm-up loosens thick oil before your first pull. That helps after a night in a cold car during a Michigan winter.
  • Rechargeable batteries. A charging port lets the battery last as long as the oil, so you aren't left with a dead battery and oil still inside.
  • Oil windows and indicator lights. A clear window shows how much oil is left, and a light can warn you when the battery is low.
  • Heat settings. Some devices offer more than one temperature, so you can start low and adjust.

Distillate, live resin and live rosin disposables

The oil inside matters as much as the hardware. Here's how the main types differ:

Oil typeHow it's madeWhat to expect
DistillateCannabis oil refined to a high concentration of cannabinoidsConsistent and potent; the flavor depends on the terpenes in the blend
Live resinExtracted from fresh-frozen plants, usually with solvents, to preserve more of the fresh flowers' original aromaA fuller, strain-like flavor
Live rosinPressed with heat and pressure from ice-water hash made from fresh-frozen plants, with no solventsSolventless and aromatic

Strain-specific disposables aim to capture one named strain's profile, while blends mix profiles for a set flavor. Our vape strain roundup covers popular profiles, and our live resin vs. live rosin guide compares those two extracts in depth.

Most THC vape oil is strong. A 2020 Canadian review found high-THC vape products were the most commonly sold and put them at 70% to 95% THC, so small puffs make sense.

Disposable vs. cartridge: which suits you?

DisposableCartridge and battery
SetupNoneMatch a cartridge to a compatible battery; many use the common 510 thread
Upfront costOne purchaseA battery once, then cartridges
Cost over timeHigher if you vape oftenOften lower once you own a battery
WasteThe whole device, battery includedMostly empty cartridges
VarietyEasy to try something newSwap cartridges on one battery
Best forOccasional use and first triesRegular use and fine-tuning

If you vape once in a while, a disposable keeps things simple. If you vape often, a quality battery with cartridges usually makes more sense over time and creates less waste. Our THC vape buying guide covers cartridges, batteries and compatibility.

Shopping for a disposable: label, testing, size and potency

  • Buy licensed. In Michigan's licensed stores, every vape arrives sealed, with its THC and CBD figures and the lab and date of its testing on the label.
  • Check the oil type and strain on the package or the menu listing.
  • Pick a size you'll finish. Disposables come in different fill sizes, and a smaller one is a sensible first try.
  • Respect the potency. Higher THC isn't better by default. If you're new, take one small puff and wait, since inhaled effects can need up to half an hour to peak.
  • Skip unlicensed vapes. During the 2019-2020 outbreak of vaping-related lung injuries known as EVALI, 2,807 hospitalized cases or deaths were reported to the CDC by February 2020. Vitamin E acetate was strongly linked to the outbreak, and THC vapes, especially ones obtained informally from friends, family or dealers, were linked to most cases. Back then, the CDC and FDA recommended against using THC vaping products, especially ones from those sources.

High Society's menus include vape pens. What's in stock differs from store to store, so look at your store's menu before you go, then reserve your picks online and collect them in the store or curbside.

Troubleshooting: clogs, charging and the last few puffs

  • Hard to pull or clogged. Warm the device in your hand or pocket, use preheat if it has it, then take a few slow, gentle draws. Hard pulls can drag thick oil into the air channel and make the clog worse.
  • Not hitting. Charge it with a compatible cable. On many devices, a blinking light means the battery is low.
  • Leaks. Store it upright and away from heat, and don't leave it in a hot car in summer or a freezing one in winter.
  • The last few puffs. As the oil runs low, the coil can heat without enough oil and taste burnt. When the flavor turns harsh, it's finished.

Our guide to clogged and leaking vapes has more fixes.

Disposal: batteries and e-waste

An empty disposable still holds a lithium-ion battery, and often a little leftover oil.

  • Keep it out of the trash and the recycling bin. The EPA says devices with lithium-ion batteries, e-cigarettes included, shouldn't go in household garbage or recycling, because damaged batteries can start fires.
  • Take it to household hazardous waste or battery collection. The EPA points to household hazardous waste programs, which many towns and counties run, often free for residents. Your city or county can tell you where to go.
  • Tape or bag it. Cover the battery contacts with non-conductive tape, or put each device in its own plastic bag, before drop-off.
  • Store it safely until then. Leftover oil is still cannabis, so keep spent devices away from kids and pets.

Valid government ID required. 21+ only, or 18+ with a medical marijuana card. Please consume responsibly.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a disposable vape last?

There's no reliable puff count, because size, how often you use it and how long each draw lasts all play a part. A one-gram device holds twice the oil of a half-gram one. A rechargeable battery helps you finish the oil instead of losing the last of it to a dead battery, and storing the device upright and away from heat keeps the oil usable.

Can you recharge a disposable vape?

Many can. Disposables with a charging port let you top up the battery until the oil runs out, but you can't refill the oil. If yours has no port, it's meant to be used until the battery or the oil is spent. Either way, take the empty device to household hazardous waste or battery collection.

Disposable or cartridge: which costs less over time?

Usually a cartridge and battery, if you vape often. You buy the battery once and replace only the cartridge, while each disposable includes a battery you'll end up discarding. A disposable still makes sense for occasional use or for trying something new. Compare the price per gram on your store's menu to see the difference.

How do you dispose of a THC vape?

Don't put it in the trash or the recycling bin, since its lithium-ion battery can be damaged and ignite. Take it to a household hazardous waste site or a battery collection point; your city or county can tell you where. Tape the battery contacts or bag the device first, and store it out of reach of kids and pets until drop-off.
