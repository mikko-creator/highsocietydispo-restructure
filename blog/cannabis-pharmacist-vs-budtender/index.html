Cannabis Pharmacists and Budtenders: Who Does What, and Where to Get Medical Advice

Some states put pharmacists behind the medical cannabis counter, while Michigan's medical program runs through a certifying physician. Here's who does what, and who to ask about your health questions.

Key takeaways

  • A cannabis pharmacist is a licensed pharmacist working in medical cannabis, typically counseling patients on use, dosing and risks.
  • Connecticut and New York build pharmacists into medical dispensing; rules differ from state to state and change over time.
  • In Michigan, a Michigan-licensed MD or DO certifies patients for a medical card, and the state's Cannabis Regulatory Agency runs the registry.
  • Budtenders know products and labels but can't give medical advice; take questions about conditions and medications to your doctor or pharmacist.

A cannabis pharmacist is a licensed pharmacist who works with medical cannabis, counseling patients on how to use products, how much to take and what risks to watch for. A few states build that role into their medical programs, and Connecticut and New York are two clear examples. In Michigan, the medical side runs through a physician: a Michigan-licensed MD or DO certifies you before the state issues a registry card. At High Society, the people who help you choose products are budtenders, trained retail staff who help with product questions. For questions about a condition or your medications, your doctor or your own pharmacist is the right call.

What a cannabis pharmacist is

A cannabis pharmacist is a pharmacist first: someone trained in how medicines work, how they interact and how they're dosed, and licensed by a state board. In a medical cannabis setting, that training gets pointed at cannabis products. Depending on the state and the job, a cannabis pharmacist may:

  • Talk with patients about how to use a product, how much to take and which side effects or risks to watch for.
  • Supervise the staff who hand products to patients.
  • Look at a patient's other medications for possible interactions.
  • Help match a format, such as an oral product, an inhaled one or a topical, to a patient's needs and comfort.

So what is a cannabis pharmacist, compared with the pharmacist at your neighborhood drugstore? Same license and training, different setting. The title usually describes a job rather than a separate credential. In Connecticut, though, pharmacists can hold a state license that covers dispensing marijuana, which shows how closely some states tie the two professions together.

National Pharmacist Day, observed each January 12, is a good reminder that the pharmacist who already knows your medication list is a smart person to ask about cannabis, whatever state you live in.

Which states require pharmacists at dispensaries, and why

States tend to follow one of two broad models. Some run medical cannabis more like a pharmacy, with pharmacists built into dispensing. Others license dispensaries staffed by trained retail workers. A dispensary pharmacist requirement is the clearest sign of the first model, and as of October 2026, two states show it plainly:

StateWhat the requirements say
ConnecticutThe state's dispensary pharmacist license, which covers acquiring, possessing, distributing and dispensing marijuana, requires an active Connecticut pharmacist license and a position with a licensed dispensary facility or hybrid retailer.
New YorkMedical cannabis at a dispensing site may be handed out only by employees 21 or older under the in-person supervision of a New York-licensed pharmacist. Counseling patients on use, administration and risks is limited to pharmacists or practitioners, or to staff working under their direct supervision.

Why build pharmacists in? These programs handle cannabis as a medicine for registered patients, so they put someone trained in dosing, side effects and drug interactions at the point where patients pick it up. New York's counseling requirement spells out the purpose: guidance on use, administration and risks comes from licensed professionals or from staff they supervise.

The details vary and change over time, so if you're a patient visiting another state, look up that state's program before you go. And don't travel across state lines with cannabis: carrying it from one state to another is prohibited under federal law.

Medical vs. adult-use cannabis: different rules, different roles

Michigan runs medical and adult-use cannabis side by side. The medical vs recreational cannabis split shapes who's involved and how much you can buy:

Federal law draws a line, too. Since an April 2026 federal rescheduling decision, marijuana covered by a state medical license sits in Schedule III, while adult-use marijuana remains in Schedule I, as of October 2026. That decision doesn't say patients may carry medical cannabis across state lines or onto federal land; as of October 2026 those questions remain unresolved, so leave it at home for those trips.

For a shopper's view of the two systems, see buying medical vs. recreational cannabis, and for the broader picture, our guide to Michigan cannabis laws.

Budtender vs pharmacist: what budtenders do, and what they can't do

Budtenders are the people on the sales floor who help you find a product. At High Society, a budtender calls you up from the waiting area, helps you choose, and passes your order to our order preparation team. Another team member reviews the order with you at checkout. We spend real time training our teams so they know the products on our shelves.

What a budtender can do:

  • Explain formats: flower, pre-rolls, vapes, concentrates, edibles and topicals.
  • Read a label with you, including THC and CBD content, the activation time and the harvest date.
  • Talk through how quickly different formats tend to kick in and how long they last.
  • Explain safe storage, including keeping products away from children and pets.
  • Answer questions about ID, store procedures and how a visit works.

What a budtender can't do:

  • Diagnose a condition or tell you cannabis will help with one.
  • Recommend a product in place of medical care.
  • Advise on interactions with your medications.
  • Tell you whether cannabis is safe during pregnancy or with a health condition.

A good budtender also knows where that line sits and will point you to a clinician when a question crosses it. For more on the role, see what a budtender is.

Where Michigan patients get medical guidance

If you're looking for medical marijuana advice in Michigan, start with the professionals who know your health. Here, that guidance begins before you ever reach a store.

  • Your certifying physician. A Michigan-licensed MD or DO must certify you for a medical card. The state's application instructions call for a medical evaluation by an active Michigan physician within the past six months. That visit is the place to talk through whether cannabis makes sense for you.
  • Your regular doctor. Even if another physician signed your certification, your primary care doctor knows your history and the rest of your medications.
  • Your pharmacist. The pharmacist who fills your medications can look for interactions with what you already take.
  • The Cannabis Regulatory Agency. The CRA runs Michigan's medical marijuana registry and licenses the businesses that serve patients.

A few questions worth raising at your certification visit:

  • How should I start, and what side effects should I watch for?
  • Does cannabis fit with the medications and supplements I already take?
  • Are there activities, like driving, that I should plan around?
  • When should I follow up if something doesn't feel right?

To start the process, see how to get a Michigan medical card. For background on cannabinoids, terpenes and product types, our patient guide is a good next read.

Questions for your doctor vs. questions for your budtender

A simple way to sort it: if the question is about your body, your health or your medicines, it's a medical question. If it's about a product, a label or how the store works, a budtender can help.

Ask your doctor or pharmacistAsk your budtender
Could cannabis interact with my medications?What's the difference between these formats?
Is cannabis a reasonable option for my condition?How much THC and CBD is in one serving?
Is it safe for me if I'm pregnant, breastfeeding, or have heart or lung problems?How soon will I feel this, and for how long?
How might cannabis affect my mental health?What does the activation time on this label mean?
Should I avoid driving or other activities given my medications?How should I store this at home?

Write your medical questions down before an appointment, and bring a list of everything you take, including over-the-counter medicines and supplements. If you're new to cannabis and pregnant or breastfeeding, managing a heart or lung condition, or taking medicines that make you drowsy, have that conversation with a clinician before your first store visit, not after.

How High Society staff help patients and first-timers

Whether it's your first visit or you're a registered patient, here's how a visit works at any of our eight Michigan stores:

  1. Bring ID. Adults 21 and over can shop with government-issued photo ID that's current. Medical patients 18 and over are welcome with a valid medical card and ID.
  2. Check in. Our security and front desk team checks you in when you arrive.
  3. Meet your budtender. When it's your turn, a budtender calls you from the waiting area and helps you land on a product that fits your taste and comfort.
  4. Order prep and review. Your budtender sends the order to our order preparation team, and a team member reviews it with you at checkout.

First-timers are always welcome, and our team is glad to slow down and explain. Want to ask a lot of questions? That's what they're there for. Frequently asked questions

Do dispensaries have pharmacists?

Some do, depending on the state. Connecticut licenses pharmacists to dispense medical marijuana, and New York requires a pharmacist to supervise medical dispensing in person. At High Society, the people who help you choose products are budtenders, trained retail staff. For medical questions, including interactions with your medications, talk with your doctor or your own pharmacist, who know your health history.

Can a budtender give medical advice?

No. Budtenders can explain formats, read labels with you and describe how products are typically used, but they can't diagnose a condition, say whether cannabis will help it, or advise on drug interactions. Those questions belong with your certifying physician, your regular doctor or your pharmacist. A budtender is a great resource for the product side once those questions are settled.

Who should I ask about drug interactions with cannabis?

Ask your doctor or your pharmacist. They know your full medication list and health history, which a store employee doesn't. Bring a list of everything you take, including over-the-counter medicines and supplements, and ask whether cannabis could change how any of them work. If you're pregnant or breastfeeding, mention that too.

What's the difference between medical and recreational dispensaries?

In Michigan, medical sales go to registered patients 18 and older who show a registry card and photo ID, with limits of 2.5 ounces a day and 10 ounces a month. Adult-use sales go to anyone 21 or older with valid ID, up to 2.5 ounces per transaction. Medical patients also have a certifying physician in the picture.
