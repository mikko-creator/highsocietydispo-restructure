Drinks, Gummies or Fast-Acting Chews: A Timing Guide

Same milligrams, different clocks. How THC drinks, fast-acting edibles and traditional edibles compare on onset, peak and duration, and how to plan around them.

By the High Society teamPublished October 24, 20268 min read

Key takeaways

  • Emulsified THC drinks usually start soonest, fast-acting edibles aim to land somewhere in between, and traditional edibles take longest.
  • In a controlled study, THC brownies took half an hour to an hour to produce effects, which then built for up to three hours.
  • Edible THC passes through the liver, which converts part of it into an active compound, and edible effects can last 4 to 12 hours depending on the dose.
  • Before a second serving, wait out the peak window: at least an hour for drinks and two to three hours for traditional edibles.
  • Read the milligrams per serving, not the package total, and stick to one format per session.

THC drinks vs edibles is mostly a question of timing. With the same milligrams, emulsified drinks usually start working soonest, fast-acting edibles aim to land somewhere in between, and traditional gummies and baked goods take the longest to arrive and can last for many hours. Exact times differ between people and products, so this guide pairs what research shows with simple planning habits.

THC drinks vs edibles: same milligrams, different clocks

Ten milligrams of THC in a seltzer and ten in a gummy are the same dose on paper. What differs is the route the THC takes into your bloodstream, and that route shapes when you feel it, when it peaks and when it fades.

As a rough guide:

  1. Drinks made with emulsified THC usually start first.
  2. Fast-acting edibles, chews and gummies built on similar technology, aim to come next.
  3. Traditional edibles, from brownies to standard gummies, usually come last and can linger for hours.

That order is a tendency, not a promise. Your own body and the product’s formula can both shift it.

How emulsified THC drinks work

THC is an oil, and oil doesn’t blend into water. Drink makers break THC into tiny droplets coated with emulsifiers so it stays spread through a water-based drink. The idea is that small droplets are absorbed more readily than THC locked in fat or baked into food.

Research on finished drinks is limited, but two kinds of evidence point the same way:

  • In a 2023 survey of 370 adults who use oral cannabis products, people who drank THC beverages said they felt effects after about 19 minutes on average, versus about 33 minutes for baked goods and gummies.
  • In a small 2025 crossover study, 14 adults took 8 mg of THC (with 8 mg of CBD) once as a self-emulsifying powder and once as standard oil drops. With the emulsified form, blood levels of THC’s main active breakdown product peaked in under an hour on average, versus about four and a half hours with oil, and peak levels were higher.

Neither study settles it. The survey relied on people’s own estimates, and the lab study tested a powder rather than a canned drink. Both still suggest emulsified THC tends to arrive sooner than THC carried in oil.

Traditional edibles: the long way through digestion

A brownie or a standard gummy has to be digested before its THC is absorbed. From the gut, THC passes through the liver, which breaks much of it down before it reaches the rest of your body, so only a small share of a swallowed dose gets through. The liver also turns part of it into 11-hydroxy-THC, an active compound in its own right.

That route explains the slow start, and the effects can linger. In a controlled study of 17 healthy adults who rarely used cannabis, THC brownies produced no effects for the first half hour to hour, and effects peaked 90 minutes to 3 hours after eating. A 2003 pharmacology review puts the usual window for swallowed THC at a 30 to 90 minute delay and roughly 4 to 12 hours of effects, depending on dose.

Dose mattered a great deal in the brownie study. The 25 mg and 50 mg brownies markedly impaired thinking and coordination, while 10 mg produced noticeable effects without measurable impairment on the study’s tests. For why the same dose can feel different from one day to the next, see our guide to why cannabis can feel stronger.

Fast-acting edibles: a middle ground

Fast-acting chews and gummies use the same basic idea as drinks: THC is processed into tiny droplets or another water-friendly form so it can be absorbed sooner. Because a chew still has to dissolve in your stomach, it’s reasonable to expect it to land between a drink and a traditional edible. Few products have been studied head to head, though, so read the package’s timing language as a starting point.

Look for wording like fast-acting or quick onset on the label. If it isn’t there, plan for traditional edible timing.

Choosing a format by plan

Think about the next several hours, not only the first one:

  • A gathering that starts soon: a low-dose drink, sipped slowly, gives you a quicker read on how you feel. Skip the alcohol.
  • A quiet night in with nothing planned: a traditional edible taken early in the evening, since its effects can run for many hours.
  • Something in between: a fast-acting edible, keeping in mind that timing varies by product.
  • An early start tomorrow: a lower dose, an earlier time, or no edible at all.
  • Anything involving driving, work or caring for others: none. Wait for another day.

Timing habits before you choose and before a second serving

A few habits make any format easier to manage:

  1. Read the label first. Check milligrams per serving and any onset wording before you open the package.
  2. Note the time. Write down when you took a serving, or set a phone timer.
  3. Know the wait. Give a drink at least an hour, and a traditional edible two to three hours, the window when the brownie study saw effects peak, before you consider more.
  4. Set your limit first. Decide your total milligrams for the night before you start, while your judgment is at its best.
  5. Start low with anything new. A new brand, format or recipe can behave differently even at the same dose.
  6. Ask about medications. THC is processed by liver enzymes that also handle many medicines, and some drugs can raise or lower THC levels. If you take medication, are pregnant or have a health condition, talk with a clinician first.

Here’s how that looks in practice. Say you eat a 5 mg traditional gummy at 7 p.m. and feel little by 8. That’s on schedule, not a dud. The earliest you’d consider more is 9 p.m., and if you feel anything by then, the answer for the night is no.

Common timing mistakes

  • Stacking servings. Feeling little at 45 minutes is normal with a traditional edible. A second piece taken then can land on top of the first one’s peak.
  • Mixing formats in one session. A drink may arrive and fade while a gummy is still building, leaving more THC at work at once than you planned.
  • Reading the package, not the serving. In Michigan’s adult-use market, an edible serving holds up to 10 mg of THC and a package up to 200 mg, while a drink serving tops out at 10 mg and a container at 100 mg. So a 100 mg bar holds at least ten servings, and eating it all at once is a mistake.
  • Adding alcohol. Our guide to THC drinks vs alcohol explains why the two don’t belong in the same night.

If you do take more than you meant to, our guide to coming down comfortably can help. New to edibles altogether? Start with our edibles basics, or bring your questions to any of our Michigan stores. 21+ only. Please consume responsibly.

Frequently asked questions

How soon will I feel a THC seltzer?

Often sooner than an edible, but it varies. In one survey, people who drank THC beverages said they felt effects about 19 minutes in, on average, compared with about 33 minutes for gummies and baked goods. Some people take much longer. Give a seltzer at least an hour before you reach for another.

When does a regular gummy usually start working?

Usually somewhere between 30 and 90 minutes after you swallow it, and sometimes later. In a controlled study of THC brownies, nothing registered for the first half hour or so, and effects built for up to three hours. Recipes and bodies differ, so plan on a slow start and a long finish.

Does a faster start mean a shorter high?

Not necessarily. Fast-acting formulas change how quickly THC is absorbed, but how long effects last depends on the dose, the product and your body. Research hasn’t settled whether drinks wear off sooner. Until you know a product, plan for the long window of a traditional edible.

Is it risky to combine a drink and a gummy in one night?

It makes timing much harder to judge. The drink may kick in and fade while the gummy is still building, so more THC can be working at once than you planned. While you learn how each format affects you, stick to one per session and count your total milligrams.

Are drinks more potent than edibles per milligram?

Milligram for milligram the dose is the same, but how much THC reaches your bloodstream can differ by formula. In a small 2025 study, an emulsified THC powder produced higher peak blood levels than oil drops at the same dose. That doesn’t mean every drink hits harder, so start low with any new product.
