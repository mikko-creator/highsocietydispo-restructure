Why Microdosing Cannabis Keeps Gaining Fans: Seven Reasons

What a microdose is, why low-dose THC keeps catching on with adults, how Michigan labels make small doses easier to measure, and the honest limits of the research.

By the High Society teamPublished October 24, 20266 min read

06min readNews & Culture

  4. Reason 3: precise low-dose products

Key takeaways

  • Researchers describe a microdose as one-fifth to one-twentieth of a typical recreational dose; against a 10 mg Michigan edible serving, that's about 0.5 to 2 mg of THC.
  • In a 2023 national survey, 9.4% of U.S. adults said they had microdosed cannabis at some point.
  • Michigan caps adult-use edible servings at 10 mg of THC, and labels list THC content, which makes small doses easier to measure.
  • Lab studies show THC's effects grow with the dose, but research on microdosing itself is still early.
  • Start low, give edibles time to peak and talk with a clinician if you're pregnant, take medications or have a health condition.

Cannabis microdosing means taking a very small amount of THC, enough for a subtle shift or sometimes nothing noticeable, instead of aiming for a strong high. It has drawn more attention as legal markets made measured doses easy to find. Here's what counts as a microdose, seven reasons it keeps gaining fans and what research does and doesn't show yet. This is educational information for adults 21 and older, not medical advice.

What counts as a microdose

There's no official definition. Researchers at the University of California San Diego and Stanford describe microdosing as taking about one-fifth to one-twentieth of a typical recreational dose. In Michigan, one adult-use edible serving can contain up to 10 mg of THC. Measured against that, a microdose works out to roughly 0.5 to 2 mg.

Some people go a little lower or higher. What matters is that the dose is small, measured and consistent, so you can tell how it affects you.

Reason 1: staying functional

Some adults want cannabis to fit into a regular day rather than take it over, and lab research shows why smaller doses appeal: effects grow with the dose. In a Johns Hopkins study of healthy adults who rarely used cannabis, a 10 mg THC brownie produced noticeable effects and a faster heart rate but didn't measurably impair thinking or coordination. Doses of 25 and 50 mg markedly impaired both.

Smaller doses mean milder effects, but any amount of THC can affect you. Never drive or operate machinery after using cannabis.

Reason 2: an easier first step for new consumers

For newcomers, a small dose is a lower-stakes way to learn how cannabis feels. That matters most with edibles. In the same study, effects didn't show up for 30 to 60 minutes and peaked 1.5 to 3 hours after eating.

Starting small and waiting before taking more helps you avoid the classic mistake of doubling up too soon. Our THC dosage guide covers serving sizes in detail.

Reason 3: precise low-dose products

Legal products make small doses easier to measure. In Michigan, adult-use edibles are capped at 10 mg of THC per serving and 200 mg per package. Labels list THC and CBD content, with a note that actual values may vary by 10%.

That makes the math simple. Cut a 10 mg piece into quarters and each quarter holds about 2.5 mg, and some products come in smaller servings to begin with. Tinctures with marked droppers and drinks with clear per-serving amounts work too. Products that pair THC with CBD in set ratios are another option, and our cannabinoid ratios guide explains how to read those labels.

Reason 4: it fits daily routines

A small, predictable amount is easier to plan around. Some adults enjoy one on a quiet evening or a weekend afternoon at home instead of saving cannabis for a big night.

A steady routine also makes it easier to notice how a given amount affects you. Keep a simple log of the product, the amount and the timing so you can adjust with care.

Reason 5: part of wider self-care habits

For some adults, a measured dose fits a broader habit of paying closer attention to what they consume. In a 2023 national survey of 1,523 adults, people who had microdosed cannabis most often gave reasons they described as medical.

Reasons aren't results, though. The survey asked why people microdosed, not whether it did anything for them.

Reason 6: less stigma, more education

Legal stores, clear labels and budtenders who can explain a serving size have made cannabis less mysterious. The same 2023 survey found microdosing was more common among people living in places with fewer restrictions on cannabis and psychedelics.

Michigan's licensed market is part of that shift. Products carry state-required labels and warnings, and stores check ID before every sale.

Reason 7: cannabis microdosing research is growing but early

Interest is growing faster than the science. Most of what we know comes from lab studies of single doses and from surveys:

  • More isn't always better. In a University of Chicago study of 42 healthy adults, 7.5 mg of THC was linked to lower self-reported distress after a stressful public-speaking task. A 12.5 mg dose increased negative mood. It was a small lab study, and it doesn't show that microdosing helps any health condition.
  • Tolerance builds. A 2018 systematic review of human research found that regular cannabis use blunts many of THC's effects over time, especially on thinking and memory.
  • Surveys show links, not causes. Microdosing was more common among people who reported poorer mental health. That doesn't mean microdosing caused it, or that it helped.

Until larger studies arrive, be skeptical of any claim that microdosing is proven to do something.

Trying it responsibly in Michigan

  • Be 21 or older, or a registered medical patient 18 or older, and buy from a licensed store.
  • Start with the smallest amount you can measure, and keep it consistent.
  • Wait before taking more. Edibles can take up to three hours to peak.
  • Skip the mix. Don't combine cannabis with alcohol or other substances.
  • Don't drive or operate a boat after using cannabis.
  • Store it safely, locked up and away from kids and pets.
  • Talk with a clinician if you're pregnant or breastfeeding, take medications or have a health condition.
  • Take breaks. If a small dose starts doing less, consider a pause; our guide to tolerance breaks explains how.

For step-by-step how-tos, read our guide to microdosing THC and CBD. If you have questions about reading a label, our budtenders at High Society's Michigan stores are happy to help, though they can't give medical advice.

Frequently asked questions

What is a microdose of THC?

There's no official amount. Researchers describe microdosing as taking about one-fifth to one-twentieth of a typical recreational dose. Measured against a 10 mg Michigan edible serving, that's roughly 0.5 to 2 mg of THC. Start at the low end and keep the amount consistent so you can tell how it affects you.

Can you feel a microdose?

Sometimes. At very low doses, some people notice a subtle change and others notice nothing at all, and sensitivity varies from person to person. Your tolerance, what you've eaten and the product all play a part. With edibles, wait the full onset time, up to three hours to peak, before deciding whether to adjust.

Is microdosing cannabis safe?

Smaller doses generally mean milder effects, but no dose is right for everyone. THC can still affect coordination, reaction time and mood, and it can interact with medications. Don't drive after using it. If you're pregnant or breastfeeding, take medications or have a health condition, talk with a clinician before you try it.

Which products make microdosing easiest?

Products with clearly measured servings. Low-dose edibles, tinctures with marked droppers and drinks with clear per-serving amounts all help, and you can split a standard 10 mg serving into smaller pieces. Inhaled cannabis is harder to measure precisely. Selection varies by store, so ask your budtender about low-dose options.

Does microdosing build tolerance?

It can. A 2018 systematic review concluded that regular cannabis use blunts many of THC's effects over time. Little research has looked at microdosing specifically, but regular use of any amount may build some tolerance. If a small dose starts doing less, consider a short break rather than a bigger dose.
