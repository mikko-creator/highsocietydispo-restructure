Clearing a Marijuana Conviction in Michigan: Expungement and Second Chances

An old cannabis conviction can follow you long after legalization. Here's how Michigan's set-aside and Clean Slate laws clear records, who qualifies and where to get free help.

07min readCannabis 101

Key takeaways

  • Misdemeanor convictions for marijuana possession, use or selling paraphernalia have their own set-aside process, and the law presumes they qualify.
  • That application has no filing fee, and the prosecutor bears the burden of proof.
  • Clean Slate has set aside many eligible convictions automatically since April 11, 2023, after 7 years for misdemeanors and 10 for felonies.
  • A set-aside conviction becomes nonpublic, so most employers won't see it, but some agencies still can.
  • Michigan Legal Help, legal aid offices and county expungement fairs offer free help.

Michigan marijuana expungement can take an old cannabis conviction out of public view, and there are two main routes. Misdemeanor convictions for marijuana possession, use or selling paraphernalia have their own set-aside process, and many other older convictions now clear automatically under the state's Clean Slate laws. Michigan's law calls it setting aside a conviction. Here's how each route works as of October 2026 and where to find free help. This is general information, not legal advice.

Why old marijuana records still matter after legalization

Michigan's adult-use law took effect on December 6, 2018, but it didn't erase earlier convictions. An old possession conviction stays on your public record until it's set aside, and it can surface in background checks for jobs and housing.

Federal rescheduling won't change that, because a Michigan court record is cleared through Michigan's own process. Our rescheduling explainer covers what federal changes do and don't do. For the wider story of past enforcement, see our look at cannabis social equity.

Michigan's marijuana set-aside for offenses that would be legal today

Michigan has a separate process for misdemeanor marijuana convictions that wouldn't be crimes today. It covers possession, use and selling marijuana paraphernalia, plus local ordinance violations that closely match them.

Filing creates a presumption that your conviction was for conduct that wouldn't be a crime on or after December 6, 2018. The prosecutor has 60 days after being served to file an answer challenging that. If none is filed, the court sets the conviction aside within 21 days.

If the prosecutor objects, the court holds a hearing within 30 days, and you don't have to present evidence. The prosecutor must show it's more likely than not that your conduct would still be a crime, and the judge rules within 14 days of the hearing.

A set-aside doesn't refund fines or costs. Felony marijuana convictions aren't covered by this process, though some may fit the general application route below.

Automatic expungement under Clean Slate: what it covers and when

Automatic set-asides under Clean Slate began on April 11, 2023. You don't file anything. Once the waiting period passes, eligible convictions in the Michigan State Police database are set aside by operation of law.

ConvictionWaiting periodLifetime limit
Misdemeanor punishable by 92 days or less7 years from sentencingNone
Misdemeanor punishable by 93 days or more7 years from sentencing4
Felony10 years from sentencing or release from state prison, whichever is later2

Some convictions never clear automatically, including assaultive crimes, crimes of dishonesty and operating-while-intoxicated offenses. Unpaid fines and costs don't block the process. A court can, however, reinstate a conviction if you haven't made a good-faith effort to pay restitution.

You won't get a notice when it happens. To check, run a public ICHAT search through the Michigan State Police for $10; it shows public records only. A personal record review ($30 plus fingerprints) also shows nonpublic entries, or you can ask the court that convicted you.

Applying yourself: steps, forms, courts and costs

If a conviction didn't clear automatically, you may be able to apply. The court forms include instructions, and Michigan Legal Help's online interview can fill them out.

Misdemeanor marijuana convictions (form MC 227a)

  1. Get a certified copy of each conviction from the court clerk. The copies may carry a fee.
  2. File a separate application in each court where you were convicted. There's no filing fee.
  3. Mail a copy to the prosecutor's office and complete the certificate of mailing.

Other convictions (form MC 227)

  1. Check eligibility. Up to three felonies and any number of misdemeanors can be set aside, but no more than two assaultive crimes in a lifetime, and some offenses never qualify.
  2. Wait 3 years for most misdemeanors, 5 years for one felony or for serious misdemeanors, and 7 years for more than one felony. The clock starts at sentencing or at the end of probation, parole or prison, whichever is last, and you can't have new convictions or pending charges.
  3. Sign the application under oath before the court clerk or a notary, and get fingerprinted at a local police agency.
  4. Send the packet, the fingerprint card and a $50 check or money order to the Michigan State Police, and mail copies to the Attorney General and the prosecutor.
  5. At the hearing, the judge weighs your conduct since the conviction and the public welfare. A set-aside by application is a privilege, not a right.

What a set-aside does and does not do

A set-aside conviction becomes nonpublic, and for most legal purposes you're treated as never having been convicted. Most employers won't see it in a background check, and background check companies can't access it.

It isn't erased, though. The Michigan State Police keep a nonpublic record that courts, police, prosecutors and a few other agencies can use for limited purposes, such as sentencing in a later case. Restitution is still owed. Immigration authorities don't recognize expungements, and the process covers only Michigan convictions. Before you answer conviction questions on a job or license application, ask a lawyer or legal aid office how they apply to you.

Job barriers, second-chance hiring and your record

A record can trip up a job search even for a minor offense, so clear what you can first. Keep two separate issues in mind:

  • Your record. Set-aside convictions stay out of most background checks, which can reopen doors a public record kept shut.
  • Drug policies. Michigan employers can still enforce drug policies and refuse to hire over a violation, even though adult-use cannabis is legal.

Some businesses call themselves second-chance employers and actively recruit people with records, and legal aid groups and local workforce agencies can point you toward them. If a conviction remains on your record, a short, honest explanation of what's changed since then can help in an interview.

Free legal help and official resources

Much of the help is free:

  • Michigan Legal Help explains the process and offers an online interview that prepares your forms.
  • Legal aid may offer free legal services if you have a low income.
  • Expungement fairs in many Michigan counties let you talk with a lawyer at no cost.
  • Your convicting court provides certified copies of your records.

Groups that work on record clearing and cannabis policy appear in our guide to cannabis advocacy organizations.

Michigan marijuana expungement checklist

  1. Look up your record through ICHAT or a personal record review.
  2. List each conviction with its court, date and charge.
  3. Check whether any already cleared automatically.
  4. For misdemeanor marijuana convictions, file form MC 227a in each convicting court and mail a copy to the prosecutor.
  5. For other convictions, check the waiting period and limits, then use form MC 227.
  6. Keep copies of everything, and get free help if a prosecutor objects.

Today's possession and use limits are in our Michigan cannabis laws guide.

Frequently asked questions

Can I clear an old marijuana possession conviction in Michigan?

Often, yes. If it was a misdemeanor for possessing or using marijuana, or for selling marijuana paraphernalia, you can apply to set it aside with form MC 227a in the court where you were convicted. The law presumes the conduct would be legal today, so the prosecutor has to prove otherwise to stop it. There's no filing fee, though certified copies of your record may cost something.

Is marijuana expungement automatic in Michigan?

Sometimes. Since April 11, 2023, Clean Slate has automatically set aside many eligible convictions, generally 7 years after sentencing for misdemeanors and 10 years for felonies, within lifetime limits. You won't get a notice, so check ICHAT, request a personal record review from the Michigan State Police or ask the court. If a conviction wasn't cleared, you may be able to apply.

Does a set-aside show up on background checks?

Usually not. A set-aside conviction becomes nonpublic, so most employers won't see it, and background check companies don't get access to it. The Michigan State Police keep a nonpublic record that courts, police, prosecutors and a few other agencies can use for limited purposes. Immigration authorities don't recognize expungements, so get legal advice if that matters to you.

Do I need a lawyer to expunge a record?

Not always. The court forms come with step-by-step instructions, and Michigan Legal Help's online interview can fill them out. A lawyer or legal aid office can still help, especially if a prosecutor objects, you have several convictions or immigration is a concern. Many Michigan counties also have free expungement events where you can talk with a lawyer.
