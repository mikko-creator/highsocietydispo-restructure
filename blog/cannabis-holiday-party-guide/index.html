Cannabis at Holiday Parties: Etiquette for Hosts and Guests

A guide for both sides of the invitation: Michigan's legal boundaries, holiday travel rules, setting the mood, low-dose options, alcohol, non-consumers and getting everyone home safely.

By the High Society teamPublished October 24, 20269 min read

Key takeaways

  • Host on private property, keep the guest list 21 and older, and check your lease or association rules before anyone lights up.
  • Never sell cannabis or charge guests for it. Adults may share it as a free gift, within Michigan's limits.
  • Cannabis cannot legally cross state lines, and TSA refers illegal finds to police, so it stays home when you travel.
  • Skip alcohol with cannabis and offer low-dose options, remembering that edibles are slow and may not register for 2 hours.
  • Settle rides before the party starts: Michigan bans driving under the influence and sets no safe THC number.

Hosting a cannabis holiday party goes smoothly when the host plans ahead and guests follow a few simple courtesies. In Michigan, adults 21 and older can gather to enjoy cannabis on private property, but the law draws firm lines around public use, driving and crossing state lines. This guide covers both sides of the invitation. Hosts get the legal basics, setup ideas and a plan for anyone who overdoes it; guests get etiquette, low-dose options and advice on alcohol and getting home.

Know the law: hosting a cannabis holiday party in Michigan

Michigan's adult-use law gives hosts plenty of room, with a few firm boundaries:

  • Adults only. The law covers people 21 and older and doesn't authorize anyone younger to use or receive cannabis. Keep the guest list, and the cannabis, strictly 21+.
  • Private property, not public places. Consuming cannabis in a public place isn't allowed, so the party stays indoors, in your yard or on other private property where you have permission. A sidewalk or a park doesn't qualify.
  • Property rights come first. Property owners can ban or limit cannabis on their property. If you rent, a landlord may ban smoking, but the lease can't stop you from lawful use that involves no smoke, such as edibles. Hotels can set their own bans, too.
  • Community rules. If you live in a condo or a neighborhood with an association, read its rules on smoking and shared spaces before the invitations go out.
  • No sales, no cover charge. Only licensed businesses may sell cannabis, and charging guests for it, directly or through a cover charge, means it's no longer a gift. Gifting between adults is fine: up to 2.5 ounces, to people 21 or older, with nothing taken in exchange. A business that charges entry or sells goods while people consume on site needs a state consumption license and local approval, so keep your party free and private.

For possession limits and everything else, read up on Michigan's cannabis rules in full.

Holiday travel with cannabis: state lines, flights, road trips

Holiday travel is where good intentions run into federal law, so keep this part simple:

  • State lines: Michigan law doesn't protect cannabis once it leaves the state, and carrying it over a state line breaks federal law, even when the other state has legalized it. As of October 2026, Indiana has no legal adult-use or medical marijuana, and bringing Michigan purchases into Illinois, Ohio or Wisconsin isn't a legal plan either.
  • Flights: TSA officers don't search for drugs, but if they find something illegal during screening, they refer it to law enforcement, and federal law still prohibits adult-use cannabis. Leave it home, and see our explainer on air travel and cannabis rules for the details.
  • Road trips within Michigan: adults may transport up to 2.5 ounces, but using it while you drive is banned, and so is lighting up inside a vehicle on a public road. Pack it sealed in the trunk, out of everyone's reach; that's the careful habit.
  • Out-of-town guests: visitors who are 21 or older can buy cannabis in Michigan with valid ID, but whatever they buy has to stay in Michigan when they head home.

Atmosphere: playlist, lighting, airflow

Cannabis-friendly parties work well when they feel easygoing rather than over-planned:

  • Playlist: build it ahead of time and keep the volume low enough for conversation. Start mellow and build as the night goes on, so nobody has to fiddle with a phone mid-party.
  • Lighting: warm lamps and string lights set a calm mood. If you use candles, put them in sturdy holders away from coats and curtains, and let a sober guest keep an eye on them.
  • Airflow: pick one well-ventilated room, a porch or a patio for smoking and vaping, and keep the rest of the home smoke-free. A cracked window and a fan do more than air freshener.
  • Comfort: plenty of seats, water within reach and snacks in every room keep people settled.
  • Something to do: a jigsaw puzzle on a side table, a deck of cards or a holiday movie playing quietly gives guests an easy activity besides conversation.

Host checklist: product variety, dose labels, locked storage, a plan if someone overdoes it

Run through this a day or two ahead:

  • Variety in small sizes. A mix of formats lets guests choose what suits them: small pre-rolls, low-dose edibles, a tincture and a few infused drinks, alongside plenty of options without cannabis.
  • Labels on everything. Mark each infused item with the word infused and the THC per serving. Keep infused and plain food on separate tables, with separate utensils. Our Friendsgiving hosting guide explains how to dose infused dishes.
  • Locked storage. Keep extra cannabis, edibles and leftovers locked away from kids, pets and curious guests; a lockbox in a closet works well.
  • A plan if someone overdoes it. Set aside a quiet room with a couch, water and light snacks. Learn the warning signs the CDC lists, among them extreme confusion, panic, paranoia, a racing pulse and severe vomiting or nausea. For advice, Poison Control is at 1-800-222-1222, and 911 is the call in an emergency. We also have ways to come down from a high that can help a guest settle.
  • A sober point person. Ask one adult to skip cannabis for the night, keep an eye on the room and handle anything unexpected.

Guest etiquette: ask first, bring your own, share respectfully

Guests have a part to play, too:

  • Ask before you bring it. Don't assume a holiday party is cannabis-friendly. Ask the host what's welcome, where consumption happens and whether kids or relatives are part of the picture.
  • Bring your own. Arrive with what you plan to use instead of counting on the host's supply. If you'd like to share, offer it as a gift, never for money.
  • Follow the house plan. Use the area the host has set aside, and keep cannabis away from the kitchen, children's rooms and anywhere neighbors are passing by.
  • Share respectfully. Offer, don't push. Say what a product is and how strong it is, and take no for an answer the first time.
  • Never spike anything. Bringing infused food or drinks without telling the host and everyone who might eat them is a hard no.

Wondering which product types suit a social evening? Our article on the best strain type for socializing walks through the options.

Low-dose options for guests

Holiday parties run long, so pacing beats potency:

  • Edibles: Health Canada's lower-risk tips suggest no more than 2.5 milligrams of THC to start. Have yours early in the evening, since effects may take 30 minutes to 2 hours to appear and up to 4 hours to peak. And don't reach for a second piece just because the first seems slow.
  • Inhaled: a puff, maybe two, of a product no stronger than 10% THC, then a break. Inhaled effects show up within minutes.
  • Drinks: in Michigan, one serving of a cannabis drink may contain as much as 10 mg of THC, which makes it a full serving, not a sip. Pour half, sip slowly and wait.
  • Microdosing: some guests prefer very small, measured amounts spread across the evening, an approach explained in our piece on small, measured servings for the holidays.

Hosts can help by setting out small pieces, small glasses and clear labels, which makes the low-dose choice the easy one.

Drinks: why mixing alcohol and THC backfires, plus mocktail ideas

Alcohol and cannabis make a poor pair at a party. The CDC's view is that using them at the same time probably leaves people more impaired than either one would, which is how a cheerful evening turns into a long night for somebody.

Make the alcohol-free bar the inviting one:

  • Cranberry-rosemary sparkler: cranberry juice, soda water and a sprig of rosemary.
  • Ginger-pear fizz: pear juice, ginger beer and a squeeze of lemon.
  • Hot spiced cider: warm apple cider with cinnamon, cloves and an orange slice, served in mugs.
  • Citrus spritz: orange and grapefruit juice topped with tonic water.

If you offer infused drinks, keep them on a separate, labeled tray, and never add alcohol to them.

Making non-consumers comfortable

Plenty of guests won't partake, and a good party works just as well for them:

  • Keep the main rooms smoke-free, and confine smoking and vaping to the area you set up.
  • Offer equally good alternatives, from mocktails to desserts, so nobody feels like an afterthought.
  • Accept a no without comment, and don't make cannabis the center of every conversation.
  • Look out for guests who get drug tested. Secondhand exposure can produce positive urine tests, NIDA notes, so a separate, well-ventilated space matters for anyone subject to workplace testing.
  • Mind family dynamics. If relatives or older guests are uneasy, keep consumption low-key and in its designated spot.

Getting everyone home safely

End-of-night planning starts before the first guest arrives:

  • No one drives after consuming. Michigan bans driving while under the influence, and there's no numeric THC limit that tells you you're safe behind the wheel. Edibles in particular can still be building hours later.
  • Line up options: sober drivers, rideshares, or couches and air mattresses for anyone who'd rather stay.
  • Hold keys if needed. A friendly host can keep keys for guests who planned to stay over.
  • Check in before people leave. Ask how each guest is getting home, and send them off with water and something to eat.
  • Watch the weather. Snow and ice on Michigan roads are one more reason to settle rides and overnight spots early in the evening.
  • Leave the cannabis behind, too. Guests heading out of state should not take any cannabis with them; carrying it across a state line isn't legal.

With that settled, enjoy the season with the people you like most. 21+ only. Please consume responsibly.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal to host a cannabis party in Michigan?

Yes, within limits. Adults 21 and older can consume cannabis on private property where it's permitted, but not in public places. Keep the guest list 21+, don't sell cannabis or charge entry, and check your lease or association rules. Adults may share up to 2.5 ounces with other adults as a gift, provided nothing is received in return.

Can I bring weed to a holiday party?

Only if the host says it's welcome. Ask first, bring your own and use it where the host has set up a space. Share it only as a gift, never for money, and don't bring infused food without telling the host and guests. And never carry cannabis across a state line on the way, since that's illegal under federal law.

What should a host do if a guest gets too high?

Move them somewhere quiet and comfortable, keep your own voice calm and offer water and a snack. No more cannabis or alcohol, and no driving. Someone should stay with them, because an edible's effects can linger for hours. If you need advice, Poison Control is at 1-800-222-1222; if symptoms look severe, call 911.

Can I drive home after a small edible?

No. Edibles are slow: effects may not appear for 2 hours and can keep rising for 4, so you might feel fine when you leave and impaired on the road. Michigan bans driving under the influence and has no numeric THC limit to go by. Plan a sober ride or stay over instead.
