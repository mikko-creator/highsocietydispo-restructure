Honoring Juneteenth: Freedom, History and the Fight for Cannabis Justice

The story of June 19, 1865, how Juneteenth grew from a Galveston celebration into a federal holiday, and why cannabis justice belongs in the conversation.

By the High Society teamPublished October 24, 20265 min read

05min readCannabis 101

  2. How Juneteenth became a federal holiday
  3. Why the day still invites reflection
  4. The war on drugs and unequal cannabis enforcement
  5. Repair efforts: expungement, equity programs and reinvestment
  6. Ways to observe Juneteenth
  7. Frequently asked questions

Key takeaways

  • Juneteenth marks June 19, 1865, when General Order No. 3 announced freedom for enslaved people in Texas, two and a half years after the Emancipation Proclamation.
  • Celebrations began in Galveston in 1866, revived during the civil rights era, spread to major cities in the early 1970s and became a federal holiday on June 17, 2021.
  • According to the ACLU, Black people's risk of a possession arrest for marijuana in 2018 was 3.64 times that of white people, though use rates were similar.
  • Clearing records, equity programs and community reinvestment are among the ways states and the federal government have tried to repair that harm.

What is Juneteenth? It marks June 19, 1865, when a Union general in Galveston, Texas, announced that all enslaved people in the state were free. Today it's a federal holiday and a day for both celebration and reflection. Here's the Juneteenth history behind it, how it became a national holiday, and why unequal cannabis enforcement belongs in the conversation.

What happened on June 19, 1865

President Abraham Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation on January 1, 1863. In Texas, freedom took far longer to arrive. On the morning of June 19, 1865, Union Major General Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston with General Order No. 3, informing the people of Texas that all enslaved people in the state were free.

That was two and a half years after the proclamation. According to the National Archives, the order delivered freedom to some 250,000 people who were still enslaved in Texas.

How Juneteenth became a federal holiday

One year later, on June 19, 1866, freed people in Galveston organized the first annual celebration of emancipation in Texas. Early gatherings also served the community in practical ways, including sharing information about voting. In 1872, four Houston church leaders bought 10 acres in the city's Third Ward to be used only for emancipation celebrations.

The tradition faced headwinds. During the Great Migration, many Black Texans moved north and west, and distance from family and factory work made the old celebrations harder to keep. Interest returned in the 1950s and 1960s, as people tied the civil rights movement to the meaning of emancipation. By the early 1970s, Juneteenth celebrations were emerging in cities such as Chicago, Los Angeles, Oakland, Seattle and Minneapolis.

On June 17, 2021, President Joe Biden signed the Juneteenth National Independence Day Act, adding June 19 to the country's legal public holidays. As of 2026, all 50 states and the District of Columbia recognize the day as a holiday or observance.

Why the day still invites reflection

Juneteenth celebrates freedom, and it also marks how long freedom was withheld from people who were already free on paper. That gap between what the law promised and what people lived through is part of why the day still resonates. It's a time to honor resilience, family and community, and to look honestly at where equality still falls short.

The war on drugs and unequal cannabis enforcement

Cannabis enforcement is one place where that gap is easy to measure, and the war on drugs' racial impact shows up plainly in arrest data. An ACLU report published in 2020 examined marijuana arrests from 2010 to 2018. Police made more than 6.1 million marijuana-related arrests over that span. In 2018, Black people were arrested for possession at 3.64 times the per-capita rate of white people nationwide, up from 3.31 in 2010, even though the two groups use marijuana at similar rates.

An arrest or conviction can follow a person for years, affecting work, housing and more long after the case closes. Our history of the war on drugs traces how those policies took shape.

Repair efforts: expungement, equity programs and reinvestment

  • Clearing records. Presidential pardon proclamations in October 2022 and December 2023 covered certain federal offenses related to marijuana possession. A presidential pardon can't reach state convictions, so state records depend on state law. If you have an old Michigan marijuana record, our Michigan expungement guide is a good place to begin.
  • Equity programs. Some states have created cannabis equity programs that offer licensing support to people and neighborhoods most affected by past enforcement. Our article on cannabis social equity looks at how these programs work.
  • Reinvestment. As of October 2026, Illinois's Restore, Reinvest, and Renew program directs 25% of the state's adult-use cannabis tax revenue to grants. They go to communities hit hardest by economic disinvestment, violence and the war on drugs.

Ways to observe Juneteenth

  • Learn. Read the history of General Order No. 3, visit a museum exhibit or attend a local history talk.
  • Support Black-owned businesses, in cannabis and beyond, and listen to the people building them.
  • Volunteer or donate with groups working on reentry, record clearing and fair policy. Our list of cannabis advocacy organizations is a good starting point.
  • Gather and celebrate. Juneteenth began as a community celebration, and sharing a meal, music and stories honors that history.

Juneteenth is one of several heritage observances on our blog, alongside Black History Month and Hispanic Heritage Month.

Frequently asked questions

What does Juneteenth commemorate?

Juneteenth commemorates June 19, 1865, when Union Major General Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston, Texas, with General Order No. 3, announcing that all enslaved people in the state were free. It came two and a half years after the Emancipation Proclamation, and it brought freedom to some 250,000 people still enslaved in Texas.

When did Juneteenth become a federal holiday?

On June 17, 2021, President Joe Biden signed the Juneteenth National Independence Day Act, which added June 19 to the list of legal public holidays. Communities had celebrated the day long before that, starting in 1866, when freed people in Galveston held the first annual emancipation celebration in Texas.

How did cannabis laws affect Black communities?

Unequally. An ACLU report found that in 2018 a Black person’s risk of a marijuana possession arrest was 3.64 times a white person’s, despite similar rates of use. Those arrests could shape jobs and housing for years, which is why record clearing and equity programs matter.
