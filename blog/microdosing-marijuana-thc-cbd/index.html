Microdosing Cannabis: How Small Doses of THC and CBD Work

A plain-spoken guide to microdosing cannabis: what counts as a microdose, how to find your lowest effective amount with a simple log, which formats measure well, and what studies show.

Key takeaways

  • A THC microdose has no official size; 1 to 2.5 mg, a fraction of a typical 10 mg edible serving, is a cautious place to start.
  • Find your amount by starting very low, keeping a log and adjusting slowly.
  • Research on low doses is small and early; it doesn’t show that microdosing reliably helps any condition.
  • Tinctures, low-dose gummies and mints, portionable drinks and single vape pulls measure small amounts better than smoking.
  • A microdose is still THC: don’t drive after using it.

Microdosing THC means taking a deliberately small amount, often around 1 to 2.5 mg, so you notice a subtle shift instead of a full high. People microdose CBD, too, though “micro” means something looser there. This guide covers how to find your personal minimum dose, the formats that measure small amounts well, and what research does and doesn’t show.

What microdosing means for cannabis

In cannabis, a microdose is a small, intentional serving of THC meant to stay below the point of feeling clearly high. There’s no official definition: guides range from about 1 to 5 mg, and researchers describe a microdose as a fifth to a twentieth of a recreational dose. Starting around 1 to 2.5 mg, a fraction of a typical 10 mg edible serving, keeps you at the cautious end. If a gummy holds 10 mg, a 2.5 mg microdose is a quarter of it, and microdose edibles made for this purpose print a small amount on each piece.

Even clinical dosing advice starts small. An international expert panel’s 2021 recommendations for doctors treating chronic pain add THC at 1 mg or 2.5 mg in their slower protocols and raise it in small steps, days apart.

With CBD, the idea is looser. CBD isn’t intoxicating the way THC is, and product strengths vary widely, so people usually mean a small, steady amount rather than a specific number.

Why people try microdosing

  • Easing in. New consumers, or people coming back after years away, can learn how THC feels without overshooting.
  • Subtler, steadier effects. A small amount lets some people enjoy a hobby, a meal or a conversation while staying clear enough to follow along.
  • Staying present at a gathering. Some adults prefer a small amount when they’re with friends, so they can follow the conversation and keep the evening easy.
  • Keeping tolerance in check. Some regular consumers keep doses small so that modest amounts keep working for them. A break is the other tool; our guide to tolerance breaks explains how they work.

Whatever the reason, the method is the same: start low, change slowly and pay attention.

Microdosing THC: finding your dose

Finding your dose is a slow, deliberate process:

  1. Start very low. Begin around 1 to 2.5 mg of THC. If you’re sensitive or new, start at the bottom of that range.
  2. Hold steady. Repeat the same amount at a similar time for a few sessions before changing anything.
  3. Adjust in small steps. If you feel nothing and want a little more, raise the amount by about 1 mg, then hold again. The slower protocols in the 2021 clinical guidance move THC up by 1 to 2.5 mg, with days between each step.
  4. Stop at the smallest amount that works. That’s your minimum effective dose, and it may be lower than you’d guess.

What should a microdose feel like? Often it’s quiet: food tastes a little brighter, music pulls you in a bit more, the evening moves at a slower pace, or you notice nothing obvious at all. If you feel clearly high, foggy or anxious, that amount sits above your microdose range.

Several things change the right amount: your tolerance, body and metabolism, whether you’ve eaten, the format and anything else in your system, from alcohol to medications. If you take any medication, talk with a clinician first.

What research says about low-dose THC, and its limits

Research on very small THC doses is early, and most studies are small.

  • Stress and mood (2017). Chicago researchers gave 42 healthy adults capsules holding 0, 7.5 or 12.5 milligrams of THC, then put them through a stressful public-speaking task. The 7.5 mg group reported less distress after the task. The 12.5 mg group reported more negative mood and performed worse. Note that 7.5 mg sits well above most microdose definitions, and the study measured one-time doses in a lab, not a microdosing routine.
  • Nerve pain (2020). In an Israeli crossover trial, 27 people with chronic nerve pain each inhaled three single metered doses on separate occasions: half a milligram of THC, 1 mg of THC and a placebo. Pain ratings dropped from their starting point after both doses, and the 1 mg dose beat placebo; side effects were mostly mild, with no consistent cognitive impairment. Each dose was one inhalation followed for 150 minutes, staff of the device maker were among the authors, and it says nothing about everyday products.

When you see a headline about low-dose THC, ask four questions: how many people took part, for how long, compared with what, and who paid for it. Those answers usually explain how much weight a finding can carry.

What these studies suggest is that low and higher doses can feel very different, and that more THC isn’t always more pleasant. What they don’t show is that microdosing reliably helps any health condition. Small samples, single sessions and lab settings limit what anyone can conclude. If you’re considering cannabis for a health concern, talk with a clinician.

Low-dose CBD: what’s known, and what isn’t

CBD isn’t intoxicating like THC, so many people start there. Here’s the current picture:

  • Approval. The FDA has approved only one CBD product, a medicine for certain rare seizure disorders, and no other CBD product.
  • Side effects. The FDA lists drowsiness, digestive upset such as diarrhea or decreased appetite, and mood changes such as irritability. It also warns that CBD can cause liver injury and can change how other medications work.
  • Full-spectrum vs. isolate. Full-spectrum CBD keeps other plant compounds, including small amounts of THC. Broad-spectrum has the THC removed, and isolate is CBD alone. If you’re avoiding THC entirely, read the label closely; our full-spectrum CBD guide explains the differences.
  • Realistic expectations. Research on small, everyday CBD amounts is thin, and you may notice little. Give a product a fair, consistent trial, and stop if you don’t like how you feel.
  • Quality. Buy from a licensed source, and check the label for the CBD and THC amounts and the lab that tested it.

Our CBD dosage guide covers amounts in more detail.

A simple microdosing routine

A routine turns guesswork into information:

  • Keep a dose log. Write down the date, time, product, amount, what you ate and how you felt at one, two and four hours.
  • Keep timing consistent. A similar time of day and setting make sessions comparable.
  • Vary only one thing per round. Try a new product or a new amount, never both at once.
  • Review each week. Look for patterns: which amount felt right, which was too much, which did nothing.

Formats that make small doses easy

FormatHow small you can goWhen you feel itNotes
TincturesBy the drop; check mg per mLVaries; swallowed, like an edibleEasy to adjust in tiny steps
Low-dose gummies and mintsA small printed amount, or half a pieceSlowly, sometimes close to two hoursExact numbers on the package
Portionable drinksPart of a can or bottleSlowly, though some are sold as fast-actingReseal and refrigerate the rest
VapesA single short pullWithin minutesDose per pull varies
Smoked flowerHard to pin downWithin minutesPuff size and burn vary

The best products for microdosing are simply the ones that let you hit the same small number every time. Smoking is the hardest format to measure. How deeply you inhale, how long you hold it, how much burns off between puffs and the THC in that particular bud all change the dose. A vape pull is a bit more consistent, but it’s still an estimate.

If precision matters to you, a tincture or a low-dose edible is the better tool. Our tincture guide and THC drinks guide cover those formats.

Common microdosing mistakes

  • Stacking doses. A second serving before the first has fully landed is the quickest way to turn a microdose into a big one.
  • Re-dosing edibles too soon. Swallowed THC can take close to two hours to be felt. Wait it out.
  • Assuming low means no impairment. A microdose is smaller, not nothing. You can’t count on a small amount leaving your reaction time untouched, so don’t drive after using THC.
  • Switching products mid-experiment. Strengths and formats differ, so keep the product steady while you dial in the amount.
  • Chasing a feeling. If a microdose feels like very little, that may be the point. Resist adding more just to feel something.
  • Adding alcohol. It muddies your notes and brings effects of its own.

Legal and practical questions

  • Hemp CBD in Michigan. Under Michigan law, cannabis with 0.3% THC or less, counting THCA, is industrial hemp, and businesses need a state license to process or sell it. Above that line, it’s marijuana, which only licensed marijuana businesses may sell.
  • Federal changes. As of October 2026, a narrower federal definition of hemp is set to take effect in late 2026, and further changes are possible. Under it, finished hemp products with more than 0.4 mg of total THC per container would no longer count as hemp, which matters for full-spectrum CBD.
  • Timelines. Inhaled THC is felt within minutes; swallowed THC is slow to start and lasts much longer, so plan microdoses around that.
  • Side effects. Higher doses bring more of them. In the Chicago study, 12.5 mg raised negative mood while 7.5 mg did not.
  • Drug tests. A microdose still puts THC in your system, and tests look for its by-products, so small amounts can still register.

Frequently asked questions

What counts as a microdose of THC?

Guides put a THC microdose anywhere from about 1 to 5 mg, and 1 to 2.5 mg, a fraction of a typical 10 mg edible serving, is the cautious end. There’s no official cutoff. In practice, it’s the smallest amount that gives you a subtle effect you enjoy without feeling clearly high, and for some people that’s 1 mg or less.

How long before I notice anything from microdosing CBD?

There’s no reliable timeline. CBD isn’t intoxicating, so any effects tend to be subtle, and research on small daily amounts is thin. Swallowed products work slowly, like edibles. Give a product a consistent trial, keep notes, and talk with a clinician first if you take medications, since CBD can change how other drugs work.

Can a microdose still impair driving?

It can, and you can’t count on it not to. People react differently, and small studies of very low doses can’t predict how you’ll respond on a given day. The safe plan is simple: if you’ve used any THC, don’t drive, and give edibles extra time to wear off before you get behind the wheel.

Is hemp-derived CBD legal in Michigan?

Yes, within limits. Michigan classifies cannabis with 0.3% THC or less, counting THCA, as industrial hemp, and businesses need a state license to process or sell it. Federal rules are shifting: as of October 2026, a narrower federal hemp definition is set to take effect in late 2026, and further changes are possible.

What are the side effects of CBD?

The FDA lists drowsiness, digestive upset such as diarrhea or decreased appetite, and mood changes such as irritability among the side effects people may notice. It also warns that CBD can harm the liver and can change how other medications work. If you take any medication, talk with a clinician before trying CBD.
