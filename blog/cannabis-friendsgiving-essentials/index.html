Hosting a Cannabis-Friendly Friendsgiving: Pairings, Etiquette and Safety

Plan a cannabis-friendly Friendsgiving: terpene pairings for classic dishes, low-dose options, mocktails instead of mixing, clearly labeled infused food and a safe ride for every guest.

Key takeaways

  • Match terpenes to dishes by aroma: limonene smells of lemon and orange, caryophyllene of black pepper and cloves.
  • Make cannabis optional, label every infused dish and never serve it to anyone who has not been told.
  • Skip alcohol with cannabis; the CDC says the combination likely impairs people more than either one alone.
  • Keep infused servings small; Health Canada's lower-risk advice tops out a first edible at 2.5 mg of THC.
  • CBD will not cancel a high, so line up sober rides or spare beds before the first guest arrives.

A cannabis Friendsgiving works best when cannabis is one option on the table, not the main event. Pair a few classic dishes with terpene profiles, keep servings small, label anything infused and give guests great alcohol-free drinks. Then make sure everyone gets home safely. This guide takes a host from pairing ideas to a morning-of checklist, for gatherings of adults 21 and older.

Why terpenes make fun food pairings

Terpenes shape much of each cannabis strain's scent (sulfur compounds add the skunky notes), and plenty of them smell like things already on a holiday table. Researchers describe limonene as citrusy, reminiscent of lemons and oranges. Caryophyllene is peppery, close to black pepper and cloves. Linalool reads as floral and lavender-like, myrcene as musky and earthy with a fruity edge, and pinene as piney and resinous.

Aroma shapes flavor, too. Limonene may make sweet foods seem a little sweeter, and caryophyllene brings a spicy note with a slight bitterness. That turns terpene food pairing into a game for anyone who loves flavor: you're matching smells, the way you'd match a sauce to a side.

Keep the game about flavor. A terpene pairing won't make a product stronger or gentler; the THC number on the label still decides how much is enough.

A pairing table: classic Friendsgiving dishes and terpene profiles

Use this as a starting point, then trust your nose. If a product's label or lab information names its main terpenes, match them to the menu. If not, a sniff of the flower usually tells you whether it leans citrus, pepper, pine or earth.

Guests who'd rather smell than consume can still play. Pass a jar of flower around the table, then seal it and put it away.

A nose-first tasting game

One of the easiest Friendsgiving ideas costs almost nothing. Set out small bowls of lemon peel, cracked black pepper with a few whole cloves, dried lavender and a sprig of pine or fir. Guests sniff each bowl, then smell a jar of flower and guess which bowl it resembles most: citrus points to limonene, pepper and clove to caryophyllene, lavender to linalool and pine to pinene. Non-consumers can join every round, because the game is all about scent.

What to have on hand: pre-roll packs, infused drinks, low-dose edibles, tinctures

A good spread offers every guest a comfortable choice, including the choice of none.

  • Pre-roll packs. Smaller pre-rolls let guests take a puff or two without committing to a full joint. Keep them in the consumption area, not on the dinner table.
  • Infused drinks. Michigan limits cannabis beverages to 10 mg of THC per serving. Serve them as mocktails, label each glass and never mix alcohol into the same cup.
  • Low-dose edibles. An adult-use edible serving in Michigan can't exceed 10 mg of THC, and many guests will want far less. Pieces that split easily help.
  • Tinctures. A dropper lets guests measure a small amount, and a mg-per-mL figure, when the label gives one, keeps the math simple.
  • CBD-forward choices. Some guests want little or no high, and a CBD-forward product can suit them from the start. It isn't a rescue for someone who's had too much THC.
  • Plenty that isn't cannabis. Water, sparkling drinks, coffee, tea and more food than seems necessary.

If you'd like a second opinion on milligrams per serving, our budtenders can compare labels with you at any of our Michigan stores.

THC and alcohol: why mixing intensifies effects, and mocktails instead

Holiday tables fill up with wine and beer, and mixing alcohol with cannabis is an easy way for a good night to go wrong. According to the CDC, taking both at once is likely to leave people more impaired than either would on its own.

There's a measurable reason. In a controlled study, adults drank a low dose of alcohol 10 minutes before vaporizing cannabis, and their peak blood THC came out significantly higher than when they vaporized without alcohol. The researchers said that may help explain why the combination impairs people more.

So choose one lane for the evening, and make the alcohol-free lane the inviting one. Seasonal mocktails:

  • Cranberry fizz: cranberry juice, sparkling water, a squeeze of lime and an orange twist.
  • Spiced cider spritz: chilled apple cider, ginger beer and a cinnamon stick.
  • Pear-thyme cooler: pear nectar, fresh lemon, a thyme sprig and club soda.

If you serve cannabis mocktails, mix them as a separate batch with a clear label and the THC per glass, and keep plain versions beside them so nobody grabs the wrong one.

Guest safety: rides home, small servings, water, CBD-forward options

A thoughtful host plans the end of the night at the start.

  • Rides home. Nobody drives after consuming. Arrange sober drivers, rideshares or a couch with spare blankets before guests arrive.
  • Small servings. Health Canada's lower-risk guidance caps a first edible at 2.5 mg of THC. For inhaling, it keeps things to a puff or two of lower-strength flower or vape oil, 10% THC or less. Edibles can take half an hour to 2 hours to show up and 4 to peak, so remind guests to wait.
  • Water and food. Keep both within reach all night. Munchies are common, and our explainer on why the munchies happen has snack ideas.
  • CBD-forward options, honestly framed. In a 2023 randomized trial, adding CBD to THC at the ratios common in products didn't reduce THC's effects. CBD won't cancel a high.
  • A plan for too much. The CDC lists signs such as extreme confusion, anxiety, paranoia, panic, a fast heart rate and severe nausea or vomiting. For calming steps, keep our tips for coming down gently handy. Poison Control (1-800-222-1222) can advise, and 911 is for emergencies.

Setting up: ventilation, a consumption area, comfort for non-consumers

The layout of your home does a lot of the hosting for you.

  • Choose a consumption area. A porch, a garage with the door partly open or one well-ventilated room keeps smoke and vapor away from the table. Renters should check the lease first.
  • Ventilate. Crack a window, run a fan and keep smoke away from the kitchen and from anyone who'd rather not breathe it.
  • Look after non-consumers. Keep the main rooms smoke-free, set out plenty of plain food and drinks, and never pressure anyone to partake.
  • Keep kids and pets out of it. This is an adults-only gathering, so store cannabis and infused leftovers where children and pets can't get to them. The CDC points out that when infants and toddlers eat cannabis by accident, they're more likely than older kids to be admitted to a hospital, partly because they're smaller.

Infused dishes: dose per serving, labels, separate serving utensils

Infused food is the serious part of hosting, because a bite can't be taken back. A few habits keep it safe:

  1. Infuse a side or dessert, never the main course. Every guest should be able to fill a plate without cannabis.
  2. Run the numbers. Estimate the total THC in your infusion, then spread it across the portions you plan to cut; our cannabutter guide shows the calculation. The result is only an estimate, since it depends on your starting material's potency and on losses during decarboxylation and infusion. Homemade batches rarely mix perfectly evenly, either, so some pieces may hold more than the average.
  3. Aim low per serving. Small, clearly portioned pieces beat one big slice.
  4. Label it. Use a card that says infused, gives the estimated THC per serving and reminds guests to wait before having more.
  5. Give it its own utensils. A spoon that wanders from the infused dish to the plain one spreads THC where it doesn't belong.
  6. Tell every guest. Never serve infused food to someone who hasn't been told what it is.

For dish ideas, see our infused holiday recipes. For etiquette beyond the food, read our tips for cannabis-friendly holiday parties.

A cannabis Friendsgiving host's checklist

Run through it the morning of:

  • Every guest is 21 or older.
  • The consumption area is set up and ventilated, away from the table.
  • Infused dishes are labeled with the estimated THC per serving and have their own utensils.
  • Plain versions of every infused item are out.
  • Mocktails, water and other non-cannabis drinks are easy to reach.
  • Low-dose and CBD-forward options are available, with honest expectations.
  • Rides home or overnight spots are planned for everyone.
  • Cannabis and leftovers are stored securely, out of reach of kids and pets.
  • Poison Control, 1-800-222-1222, is saved in your phone.

A sample evening timeline

Here's one way to run a late-afternoon Friendsgiving:

  • 3 p.m.: guests arrive to appetizers, mocktails and water, and the consumption area opens for anyone who wants a puff before dinner.
  • 4 p.m.: dinner, with infused sides labeled and served separately.
  • 5:30 p.m.: dessert. If there's an infused dessert, serve small, labeled portions now, so effects arrive while everyone is still settled in.
  • 6:30 p.m. onward: games, leftovers and conversation. Check on anyone who tried an edible, and keep the water flowing.
  • Before anyone leaves: go over the ride plan. Anyone who consumed stays over or heads home with a sober driver.

That leaves only the gravy. Enjoy the day, and please consume responsibly.

Frequently asked questions

How do you host a cannabis-friendly Friendsgiving?

Keep it adults-only, offer cannabis as an option rather than the centerpiece and label anything infused. Set up a separate, ventilated spot for smoking or vaping, put out plenty of food, water and mocktails, and keep servings small. Before guests arrive, line up a sober ride or a place to stay for every person who might consume.

Is it safe to mix cannabis and alcohol?

It's best avoided. The CDC says using both at once is likely to impair you more than either alone, and a controlled study found that a low dose of alcohol before vaporizing cannabis led to significantly higher peak blood THC. Serve mocktails alongside cannabis, and keep any infused drinks free of alcohol.

How do I dose infused stuffing or desserts?

Estimate the total THC in your infusion, divide by the number of servings and aim low, keeping a first-timer's portion at or below 2.5 mg of THC. The figure is an estimate, because potency and infusion losses vary. Infuse a side or dessert rather than the main dish, label the amount per serving and use separate utensils.

What do I do if a guest gets too high?

Stay calm and help them to a quiet place to sit or lie down, with water and a light snack. Offer no more cannabis or alcohol, and don't let them drive. Stay nearby, since edible effects can last for hours. Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 can advise, and call 911 in an emergency.
