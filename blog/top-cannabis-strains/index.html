The Cannabis Strains Worth Knowing: Classics and Modern Standouts

From OG Kush and Blue Dream to Gelato and Runtz: where well-known strains come from, how they smell, what people report and how to find a close match.

By the High Society teamPublished October 24, 20268 min read

Key takeaways

  • This list favors strains with well-documented lineage that stay on menus year after year; it isn't a sales ranking.
  • Lineage, terpenes and the THC and CBD on the label tell you more than an indica or sativa tag.
  • The same strain name can vary from grower to grower and even from batch to batch.
  • If a strain isn't on your store's menu, ask for one with similar parents or a similar terpene profile.

The top cannabis strains are the names you keep hearing: classics like OG Kush, Sour Diesel and Blue Dream that shaped today's market, and newer crosses like Gelato, Wedding Cake and Runtz that shoppers ask for by name. For each one, you'll find its commonly listed lineage, its aroma, the terpenes often reported and the effects people commonly describe. You'll also learn how to read a strain card, why the same name can vary and how to find a strain, or a close match, on High Society's menu.

How We Chose These Top Cannabis Strains

Two things earned a strain a spot here. First, documented genetics: a lineage that breeders and growers broadly agree on. Where a strain's origin story is disputed, we say so. Second, wide availability: the strain has been grown widely enough, for long enough, that you're likely to see it or its offspring on a menu.

This isn't a sales ranking, and it doesn't try to rank the most popular cannabis strains. The effects listed are what people commonly report, and they shift with dose, setting and your own body. Strain names are examples, not a stock list; each store's shelves are different and turn over often.

The Classics: Strains That Built the Modern Market

Most of these classic strains have been around for decades, and many of today's popular crosses descend from them.

OG Kush

Its origin is debated. The most common story traces it to Chemdawg crossed with a Hindu Kush-type plant, and it spread from Florida to California in the 1990s. Expect fuel, pine and lemon, with myrcene, limonene and caryophyllene often reported. People commonly describe a relaxed, happy high, and OG Kush sits in the family trees of countless newer strains.

Sour Diesel

An East Coast strain from the early 1990s whose exact lineage is still argued over; Chemdawg and Super Skunk are the parents most often named. The aroma is sharp diesel with citrus, and caryophyllene, myrcene and limonene are often reported. Reports lean energetic and talkative.

Blue Dream

Blueberry crossed with Haze, from California. It smells of sweet berry, with myrcene and pinene often reported. Many people describe it as gentle and balanced, one reason it has stayed on menus for so long.

Girl Scout Cookies (GSC)

OG Kush crossed with Durban Poison, bred in the San Francisco Bay Area. Sweet and earthy, with caryophyllene and limonene often reported. People describe a euphoric, relaxed high, and GSC is a parent of Gelato and many other dessert-named strains.

Granddaddy Purple

Usually listed as Purple Urkle crossed with Big Bud (accounts differ), introduced in California in the early 2000s. Grape and berry aromas, with myrcene often reported. It's known for deep purple color and a heavy, relaxing reputation.

Northern Lights

Descended from Afghan and Thai landrace plants and spread worldwide by Dutch seed companies in the 1980s. Sweet, earthy and piney, it's commonly described as mellow, and it's a parent of other classics, including Jack Herer.

Jack Herer

Haze crossed with Northern Lights #5 and Shiva Skunk, released by Dutch breeders in the 1990s and named for the cannabis activist and author. Pine, spice and citrus, with terpinolene often reported. Reports lean clear-headed and upbeat.

Durban Poison

A landrace sativa from the Durban region of South Africa. Sweet, earthy and herbal, with terpinolene often reported. Many people describe it as energizing, and it's one of GSC's parents.

White Widow

Bred in the Netherlands in the 1990s from a Brazilian sativa and a South Indian indica. It's known for a heavy coat of white trichomes and an earthy, woody aroma, and people describe a balanced, euphoric high.

Reading a Strain Card: Lineage, Terpenes and Reported Effects

Menus and strain guides tend to show the same few details:

  • Lineage. The parents hint at aroma and feel. A strain with GSC in its family tree often leans sweet and earthy; Haze descendants often lean bright and herbal.
  • Terpenes. These aroma compounds shape smell and flavor. Some labels list the dominant ones; our guide to cannabis terpenes explains each.
  • THC and CBD. Michigan labels must show both, along with the testing lab and test date. Higher THC isn't the same as higher quality; our list of high-THC strains puts the numbers in context.
  • Reported effects. These come from what people describe, not from a lab, so read them as a rough guide.
  • Indica, sativa or hybrid. Convenient labels, weak predictors. Genotyping work published in 2021 showed that indica- and sativa-labeled samples couldn't be told apart genetically; what separated them was a small set of terpenes. Our indica vs. sativa guide digs deeper.

Why the Same Strain Varies by Grower

No official registry ties a strain name to specific genetics, so two growers can sell genetically different plants under one name. Seeds produce plants with different traits, and even clones can pick up small genetic changes as they're copied again and again. Light, nutrients, harvest timing and how the buds are dried, cured and stored all shape what ends up in the jar.

Research backs this up. A 2019 genetic study of strains bought from dispensaries in three states found inconsistencies within strains, with most strains including at least one genetic outlier. That's why the batch in front of you, with its own label and test date, matters more than the name. Our guide to spotting premium flower shows what to look for.

Finding a Strain or a Close Match on High Society's Menu

Search your store's online menu by strain name, or browse the flower and pre-roll categories. Menus differ by store and turn over quickly, so a strain you saw last month may be gone today. You can search your store's menu online, then place an order to collect in store or curbside.

If the strain you want isn't available, ask a budtender for a close match. Describe it by:

  • its parents, such as "anything from the Cookies family?"
  • its dominant terpenes, such as "something high in limonene"
  • its aroma, such as "fuel and pine" or "sweet berry"
  • the experience you're after and how strong you want it

Want to judge the brands behind the strains, too? See our guide to Michigan cannabis brands.

Building Your Own Shortlist

  1. Start with two or three classics that differ, such as Blue Dream, Granddaddy Purple and Jack Herer.
  2. Note the grower, the harvest or test date, the THC and CBD, and the terpenes if listed.
  3. Write down how each one smelled, tasted and felt, and what time of day you enjoyed it.
  4. Branch out by lineage: if you liked GSC, try Gelato or another Cookies cross.
  5. Buy small amounts of anything new and start low, especially with high-THC modern crosses.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most popular cannabis strain?

There's no single answer. Popularity shifts from season to season and store to store, and it's rarely measured in public. Classics like Blue Dream, OG Kush and GSC have stayed in circulation for years, and newer crosses like Gelato and Runtz are names shoppers often ask for. Your store's menu shows what's available today.

What are the best strains of all time?

That comes down to taste, but a few classics earned their reputations by shaping much of what came after. OG Kush, Northern Lights, Haze descendants such as Jack Herer, and GSC turn up again and again in the family trees of newer strains. Start with one or two and see which profile you enjoy.

Why does the same strain feel different from different growers?

Because a strain name doesn't lock in the genetics. Growers may work from different seeds or clones, and the grow room, harvest timing, drying and storage all leave a mark on the final product. A 2019 genetic study found inconsistencies among samples sold under the same strain name. Your dose and your own body matter too.

How do I find a specific strain at High Society?

Search your store's online menu by name, or ask a budtender in store. Each store's selection is different and changes often. If the strain isn't available, ask for a close match by lineage, dominant terpenes or aroma; a budtender can usually point you to something similar.

What strain is good for beginners?

Many beginners start with a moderate-THC strain and a very small amount. A balanced or CBD-rich strain such as Harlequin keeps the THC lower, and Blue Dream is often described as gentle. Effects differ between people, so take one small puff, wait, and decide from there.
