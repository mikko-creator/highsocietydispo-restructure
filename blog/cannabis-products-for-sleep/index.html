Cannabis and Sleep: How to Choose Products for Your Nighttime Routine

Some adults feel drowsy after cannabis, but the sleep research is thin. Here's how edibles, tinctures and vapes differ at night, and what's really known about CBN, CBD and terpenes.

Key takeaways

  • Sleep research is limited: a 2022 review of 39 trials found small benefits, mostly in people with chronic pain, along with more dizziness.
  • Format matters more than strain name: edibles start slowly and last longest, tinctures adjust in small steps, and inhaled cannabis starts fast and fades sooner.
  • CBN isn't proven to bring on sleep; in a 2026 sleep-lab trial it didn't significantly change the main measure.
  • Nightly use can lead to dependence, and withdrawal can bring insomnia and strange dreams, so talk with a clinician about ongoing sleep trouble.

There's no best cannabis for sleep that research can point to. Some adults feel drowsy after THC, but clinical evidence is limited, measured benefits have been small, and nightly use can backfire through dependence and withdrawal. In the evening, format matters more than strain name. Edibles start slowest and last longest, tinctures adjust in small steps, and vapes or flower start fast and fade sooner. Talk with a clinician about ongoing sleep problems, especially if you take other medications.

Is there a best cannabis for sleep? What research says, and its limits

A useful summary comes from a 2022 review of randomized trials in the journal Sleep. It pooled 39 trials with about 5,100 patients: 38 tested oral cannabinoids, only one used inhaled cannabis, and most enrolled people living with chronic pain. In that group, cannabinoids probably improved sleep quality a little, while raising the risk of dizziness, with smaller increases in sleepiness, dry mouth, fatigue and nausea.

That leaves real gaps. Few trials looked at people whose main problem is insomnia, and controlled doses of oral cannabinoids aren't the same as a gummy or a pre-roll at bedtime. So it's fair to say some people feel sleepy after cannabis, and not fair to call it a proven sleep aid. If poor sleep keeps up or starts affecting your days, a clinician can look for causes cannabis won't touch.

Indica, sativa, hybrid: why labels aren't enough

Choosing an indica for sleep is a habit for many people, but the label doesn't reliably describe a plant's genetics. A 2021 genetic study found indica- and sativa-labeled samples genetically indistinct. The indica tag did track most closely with myrcene, the terpene with the sleepiest reputation, though that reputation rests mostly on early research.

The package says more than the category does. Michigan labels list THC and CBD content and an activation time. Start with those numbers, then weigh how you've reacted to similar products before.

Edibles at night: timing and duration

If you're looking at edibles for sleep, timing is everything. Health Canada puts the start of an edible's effects anywhere from half an hour to two hours after eating, with full effect taking up to 4 hours. The effects can stretch to 12 hours, and some can linger for a full day. NIDA notes that because edibles act slowly, people may take more before the first dose arrives, raising the risk of unpleasant or serious effects.

  • Take it well before you want to be winding down, not at lights-out.
  • Start small. Health Canada suggests new consumers look for edibles at or below 2.5 mg of THC.
  • Hold off on a second dose while you wait; give the first one its full window.
  • Plan for a slow morning, and don't drive until you feel completely clear.

For more background, see our guide to cannabis and sleep.

Tinctures: adjustable doses

A tincture is cannabis extract in liquid form, measured with a marked dropper. People choosing a tincture for sleep usually like that they can take a small, precise amount and change it by a fraction next time. Swallowed, a tincture follows edible-style timing. Some people hold it under the tongue first; how fast that works varies, so time it like an edible until you know how it affects you. Check the label for THC and CBD per milliliter, and keep tinctures locked away from children and pets.

Vapes and flower: faster onset, shorter effect

Inhaled cannabis works fastest. Health Canada says effects begin within seconds to minutes and reach full strength within 10 to 30 minutes, lasting up to 6 hours, though some residual effects can linger up to 24 hours. That quick feedback makes it easier to stop after a puff or two.

The trade-off is a shorter window. Wait the full half hour before taking more; Health Canada notes that taking more during that window raises the risk of adverse effects. If your evening is more about unwinding than sleeping, see cannabis for late nights.

Cannabinoids people look for: THC, CBN, CBD

THC is the main intoxicating cannabinoid. Sleepiness is one reported effect, but people react differently, and taking more raises the odds of unwanted effects.

CBN is marketed hard, and CBN for sleep is a common pitch, but the evidence is thin. In a 2026 sleep-lab trial in Sydney, 20 adults with diagnosed insomnia each spent separate nights on a single oral dose of 30 mg of CBN, 300 mg of CBN or a placebo. Neither dose significantly changed the main measure, time spent awake after first falling asleep. The 300 mg dose shifted a few secondary measures, and the researchers called for larger, longer trials. Be wary of any product that promises sleep. More on this cannabinoid: what CBN is.

CBD doesn't produce a high. Many products pair it with THC, so check how much of each is in a serving. If you take any sleep aid or other medication, talk with your clinician before adding cannabis, and don't combine cannabis with alcohol.

Terpenes people look for: myrcene, linalool

Terpenes shape a strain's smell, and two get most of the bedtime attention:

  • Myrcene has an earthy, musky aroma and is the terpene most associated with indica labels.
  • Linalool gives lavender its floral scent and turns up in some cannabis strains.

Their reputations run ahead of the science. A 2024 review described the evidence linking myrcene with relaxation, and linalool with sleep, as exploratory, and called the idea that terpenes boost cannabinoids' effects unproven. If you enjoy an earthy or floral strain at night, fine, but don't count on the terpene to do the work. More on each: myrcene and linalool.

Tolerance, dependence and sleep habits

Using cannabis every night changes the picture. NIDA notes that daily or near-daily use is linked to cannabis use disorder, and that how often someone uses is the strongest predictor of it. If you've used a lot, or for a long time, quitting or tapering can bring withdrawal symptoms such as insomnia and strange or unsettling dreams, so a nightly habit can make sleep harder just when you try to cut back.

Use it some nights rather than every night. Keep a steady wake-up time and a cool, dark room, and go easy on late caffeine and alcohol. If you've used nightly for a while and want to stop, expect sleep to be unsettled for a stretch, and talk with a clinician if it doesn't improve.

Questions to bring to a budtender, and to your clinician

Our budtenders at any of our eight Michigan stores can explain formats and help you read a label. Good questions at the counter:

  • How much THC, CBD or CBN is in one serving?
  • What activation time does this label list?
  • Can this edible be split into smaller pieces?

Save these for your clinician or pharmacist:

  • Could cannabis interact with my medications, including any sleep aid?
  • Is cannabis a good idea for me if I'm pregnant, breastfeeding, or have a heart or lung condition?

Cannabis is for adults 21 and over, or 18 and over with a Michigan medical card. 21+ only. Please consume responsibly.

Frequently asked questions

What's the best cannabis product for sleep?

Research doesn't single one out. Trials so far show small benefits at best, mostly in people with chronic pain. If you use cannabis in the evening, choose the format by timing: edibles start slowly and last longest, tinctures fine-tune a dose, and inhaled cannabis starts fast and fades sooner. See a clinician about recurring sleep problems.

Does CBN make you sleepy?

It isn't proven to. In a 2026 sleep-lab trial of 20 adults with insomnia, single doses of 30 mg and 300 mg of CBN didn't significantly change the main measure, time awake after first falling asleep, though the higher dose moved a few secondary measures. Researchers want larger, longer studies, so be cautious about CBN sleep claims.

How early should I take an edible before bed?

Give it plenty of time. By Health Canada's timeline, an edible can need half an hour to two hours to begin acting and up to 4 hours to peak, and its effects can run as long as 12 hours. Take it well before bedtime, start with part of a serving, and resist a second dose while the first is still building.

Is indica better for sleep?

Not reliably. A 2021 genetic study found that indica- and sativa-labeled cannabis was genetically indistinct, with the labels tracking a few aroma compounds such as myrcene. Some people prefer indica-labeled strains at night, but the label doesn't measure drowsiness. The THC and CBD numbers, the format and your own experience tell you more.

Can using cannabis every night affect sleep?

Yes, it can. Daily or near-daily use is linked to cannabis use disorder, and after heavy or prolonged use, withdrawal can mean insomnia and strange or unsettling dreams, which makes sleep harder when you cut back. Taking nights off and keeping steady sleep habits shows you how you sleep without it. Talk with a clinician if trouble continues.
